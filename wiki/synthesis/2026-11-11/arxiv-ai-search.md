## 1. User Embeddings are Superpositions of Interpretable Behavioral Modes
- **arXiv:** [2611.08487v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08487v1) (submitted 2026-11-05, cs.LG)
- **Authors:** Jonathan Kozaczuk, Zheng Dong, Noyan Evirgen, Keivan Majidi, Eddie K. H. Ng, Chris Porter, Qi Zhao, Jia Geng, Changmao Li, Brandon R. Feng
- **Institution/Company:** Not specified on arXiv abstract page
- **Abstract:** User embeddings are central components of modern recommendation, personalization, and behavioral modeling systems. Though these embeddings can compactly encode rich behavioral information, they are inherently dense and opaque, limiting interpretability. To address this gap, we argue that user embeddings can be understood as sparse superpositions of interpretable behavioral modes. This structure is directly predicted by generative models of user behavior for linear embeddings and extends approximately to deep-learning representations by the superposition hypothesis. Superimposed modes can naturally be recovered by sparse dictionary learning methods, such as sparse autoencoders (SAEs), and recovered modes can be automatically named and queried in natural language. We validate mode recovery against known ground truth on synthetic data, where SAEs recover modes better than classical sparse baselines, including on transformer-encoded embeddings. We then apply the method to classical and transformer-based user embeddings from two real-world datasets. We also show how the corresponding natural-language labels enable zero-shot audience retrieval and characterization over embeddings never trained on text. The framework is post-hoc and model-agnostic, requiring no modification to the upstream embedding system.
- **Key innovations:**
  - Frames user embeddings (recommendation/personalization) as sparse superpositions of interpretable behavioral modes, connecting to the superposition hypothesis.
  - Uses sparse autoencoders (SAEs) for post-hoc mode recovery, outperforming classical sparse baselines on synthetic ground-truth and transformer-encoded embeddings.
  - Enables automatic natural-language naming/querying of recovered modes and zero-shot audience retrieval/characterization over embeddings not trained on text.
  - Model-agnostic interpretability layer requiring no changes to upstream embedding pipelines.

## 2. SwarmEvolve: Latent Agent Swarms for Evolutionary Discovery and Optimization
- **arXiv:** [2611.08483v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08483v1) (submitted 2026-11-05, cs.AI/cs.NE)
- **Authors:** Qile Jiang, George Em Karniadakis
- **Institution/Company:** Not specified on arXiv abstract page
- **Abstract:** LLM-guided evolutionary search uses AI agents to improve programs for mathematical and computational problems by iteratively refining solutions and combining high-scoring methods. However, the process is often slow because the LLM generates a long textual chain of thought for each round and must repeat this generation many times to maintain population diversity. We present SwarmEvolve, a framework in which hundreds of low-cost agents per round work in swarms, reason in latent space without decoding to text, and share information by passing KV caches. Each swarm studies the problem from a different angle. A novelty filter keeps the most distinct agent ideas by directly measuring the distances between the agents' hidden state vectors. Crossover of ideas happens naturally in latent space, as a summarizer agent receives the combined KV caches of all swarm members. On 21 problems in mathematical discovery and scientific computing, SwarmEvolve gets the highest average score and needs a median of only 3 to 8 minutes to reach the best scores that other state-of-the-art frameworks reach in 60 minutes. On several problems, SwarmEvolve with Qwen3 8B improves on the solutions that AlphaEvolve first found with frontier LLMs. Our work shows that agent swarms that communicate in latent space offer a fast, low-cost approach to program search for scientific problems.
- **Key innovations:**
  - Replaces textual chain-of-thought evolutionary loops with latent-space agent swarms (hundreds of low-cost agents) communicating via KV-cache sharing.
  - Introduces hidden-state-distance novelty filtering and latent crossover through a summarizer agent over combined KV caches.
  - Achieves strong efficiency/quality tradeoff (median 3–8 minutes to match 60-minute baselines on 21 problems) and can surpass prior frontier-LLM-discovered solutions with smaller models (e.g., Qwen3 8B).

## 3. Scientist World: Grow a Research World for AI-Scientists
- **arXiv:** [2611.08478v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08478v1) (submitted 2026-11-05, cs.AI/cs.CL/cs.LG)
- **Authors:** Taiming Lu, Sachin Konan, Zhuang Liu
- **Institution/Company:** Not specified on arXiv abstract page
- **Abstract:** AI-Scientists arrived, but a world for them to grow in did not. Human scientists take theirs for granted — centuries of open problems and shared ideas, waiting in the scientific literature. To AI-Scientists, that world is readable but not runnable, so we build Scientist World for them: every research paper becomes an executable environment. Our autonomous pipeline extracts each paper's open-ended research question and reproduces its proposed method as the baseline; an AI-Scientist entering the environment is evaluated against what the human authors achieved. We release the first 1,000 verified environments. With the world as an evaluation, we found that frontier AI-Scientists can already surpass the human researchers 74% of the time. As a record of how AI does science, it contains 6,400 hours of research traces, with every idea, experiment, failure, and success. As a training ground, we are able to raise (+158%) a small model to match frontier models in research ideation. At last, AI-Scientists' own world has arrived, grown from and growing with science itself.
- **Key innovations:**
  - Converts research papers into executable environments (problem extraction + reproduced baseline) for training/evaluating AI scientists.
  - Releases 1,000 verified environments and 6,400 hours of research traces capturing end-to-end scientific process signals.
  - Demonstrates both evaluation utility (frontier agents surpass human baselines 74% in reported setting) and training utility (+158% ideation improvement for a small model).

## 4. Unpacking the Compute Efficiency of Strong-to-Weak OPD against GRPO
- **arXiv:** [2611.08453v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08453v1) (submitted 2026-11-05, cs.LG/cs.CL)
- **Authors:** Zeyu Leo Liu, Yang Li, Bo Pang, Emre Can Acikgoz, Xuan-Phi Nguyen, Anurag Koul, Srijan Bansal, Erik Nijkamp, Shafiq Joty, Semih Yavuz
- **Institution/Company:** Not specified on arXiv abstract page
- **Abstract:** On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student on its own rollouts under a frozen teacher's per-token supervision. Does this dense signal reduce the training FLOPs needed to reach a target accuracy compared with reinforcement learning? We systematically compare vanilla reverse-KL OPD with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) across task difficulty, student and teacher size, domain, and optimization choices, measuring held-out accuracy against training FLOPs. With an external post-trained teacher, OPD generally loses to GRPO in accuracy per training FLOP at matched settings, even when the teacher is further trained with GRPO, and often suffers response-length inflation. Yet a different teacher changes the picture: when the teacher is the student's own GRPO checkpoint, OPD recovers that checkpoint's accuracy on math with 50× fewer distillation FLOPs than GRPO needs to reach the same accuracy. Following the same teacher setting, we consistently observe this high efficiency with a larger student, on code, and in a second model family. On a single domain, however, the GRPO checkpoint is already the desired model, so distilling it adds to total compute. Across domains, separate GRPO checkpoints hold different capabilities, and no single checkpoint serves every domain. Multi-teacher OPD consolidates per-domain GRPO experts into a single student that matches each expert's accuracy, with the distillation stage costing only 5.2% (on 2 domains) and 8.0% (on 4 domains) of the specialist training FLOPs. Counting specialist training and distillation together, this pipeline reaches joint GRPO's performance on the 2-domain mix with 42.0% fewer total FLOPs.
- **Key innovations:**
  - Systematic FLOPs-vs-accuracy comparison of reverse-KL on-policy distillation (OPD) against GRPO across tasks, scales, domains, and optimization choices.
  - Identifies a key regime split: external teachers often underperform GRPO per FLOP, but self-distilling from a student’s own GRPO checkpoint can recover accuracy with ~50× fewer distillation FLOPs.
  - Proposes/validates multi-teacher OPD to consolidate domain-specialist GRPO experts into one model, substantially reducing total compute in multi-domain settings.

## 5. A Hidden Markov Preference Model for Online Prediction of Adversary Actions
- **arXiv:** [2611.08445v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08445v1) (submitted 2026-11-05, cs.LG)
- **Authors:** Angel Daruna, Michael Cogswell, Tixiao Shan, Yi Yao, Han-Pang Chiu, Ajay Divakaran
- **Institution/Company:** Not specified on arXiv abstract page
- **Abstract:** A defender anticipating an attacker needs a probability distribution over the attacker's next action from information available before that action occurs. Two structures complicate this: the attacker's behavioral regime persists and can switch within an episode, and each action combines an operation with state-dependent arguments whose feasible values change over time. We capture both with a hidden Markov model whose latent state is the active regime and whose regime-conditioned action law is defined on a feasibility-masked tree of executable actions. The same likelihood forms the next-action distribution and updates the regime belief once the action is seen, and a partially observed prefix contributes its resolved local factors. Estimation separates into convex logistic fits that Baum-Welch reuses when regimes are unlabeled. We derive a reach-weighted nodewise KL expression for regime information, a sequence-wise log-loss bound for switching regime paths, and a uniform fallback guarantee. On the CAGE 2 cyber defense benchmark the filtered latent state is worth 0.8 nats per step, and after a controller switch the loss begins to fall in three steps against ten for a recurrent baseline.
- **Key innovations:**
  - Models adversary behavior as latent regime-switching HMM with feasibility-masked tree-structured action space (operation + state-dependent arguments), relevant to sequential/game-theoretic prediction.
  - Unifies next-action prediction and online belief updates in one likelihood framework; supports partially observed prefixes and convex/Baum-Welch estimation.
  - Provides theoretical characterizations (regime information, log-loss bounds, fallback guarantee) and demonstrates faster adaptation after controller switches than a recurrent baseline on CAGE 2.

## 6. Understanding AI from the Inside: The State of Mechanistic Interpretability for AI Safety
- **arXiv:** [2611.08438v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08438v1) (submitted 2026-11-05, cs.CL/cs.AI/cs.LG; v2 available 2026-11-09)
- **Authors:** Fazl Barez, Tung-Yu Wu, Hunar Batra, Gabriele Dominici, Florent Draye, Yanai Elazar, Damiano Fornasiere, Antía García, Abir Harrasse, Riley Harris, Sara Hooker, Michael Lan, Belinda Z. Li, Aaron Maiwald, Narmeen Fatimah Oozeer, Naomi Saphra, Or David Shafran, Adi Simhi, Robert Trager, Shriyash Kaustubh Upadhyay, Ziyu Yao, Yu Zhao, Shay B. Cohen, Mor Geva, Yoshua Bengio, Stella Biderman, Hassan Sajjad
- **Institution/Company:** Not specified on arXiv abstract page
- **Abstract:** Neural networks are likely to be among the most consequential artefacts in human history. As these systems become more capable and autonomous, their potential to cause societal harm also grows, making AI safety one of the defining challenges of our time. Yet we continue to build increasingly powerful systems without fully understanding how they work, how they will behave in unfamiliar situations, or how to reliably control them. Mechanistic interpretability offers a path towards understanding these systems by studying their internal representations and computations, with the aim of evaluating, predicting, and controlling behaviour. We survey mechanistic interpretability for AI safety in order to answer the question: Does understanding how models work actually make them safer? We place mechanistic interpretability in the broader context of interpretability research and compare its mechanistic evidence with behavioural evaluation. We introduce the foundations of mechanistic interpretability, including features, polysemanticity, transformer computation, and circuits, and survey methods for feature extraction and analysis, circuit discovery and causal verification, explaining intermediate representations in natural language, learning dynamics, and training-data attribution. Beyond surveying existing methods, we propose a unified framework for evaluating mechanistic interpretability claims and characterise six research orientations by their goals, assumptions, and trade-offs. Finally, we examine how mechanistic understanding can support AI safety, including knowledge editing, steering, and monitoring, and assess its limitations, failure modes, open problems, and relationship to behavioural safety measures. We also release a repository of code notebooks with worked examples that reproduce key findings from surveyed methods, allowing readers to build intuition through hands-on experimentation.
- **Key innovations:**
  - Comprehensive survey of mechanistic interpretability for AI safety spanning features/circuits, causal verification, representation explanation, learning dynamics, and training-data attribution.
  - Proposes a unified framework for evaluating mechanistic interpretability claims and taxonomizes six research orientations by goals/assumptions/trade-offs.
  - Connects mechanistic understanding to safety interventions (editing, steering, monitoring) while critically assessing limitations, failure modes, and relation to behavioral evaluation; includes reproducible notebooks.

## 7. MedGraph: Maintaining Patient State with Graph Memory for Long-Term Medical Dialogue
- **arXiv:** [2611.08433v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08433v1) (submitted 2026-11-05, cs.CL/cs.AI)
- **Authors:** Donghua Zhang, Zizheng Wang, Ze Dong, Yiding Feng, Siyu Qian
- **Institution/Company:** Not specified on arXiv abstract page
- **Abstract:** LLM-based medical assistants that follow a patient across visits need a memory that keeps the patient's clinical state correct as it changes. We examine the errors of five memory systems on longitudinal medical dialogue and find three recurring causes: the retrieved evidence gives an outdated state, includes another person's health facts, or lacks a safety fact the answer depends on. We present MedGraph, a graph memory built for these three cases. Construction extracts dated facts from each session, attributes each fact to the person it describes, and links medication, diagnosis, and measurement records into chains that keep earlier states. Retrieval ranks the patient's chains as units, reads each chain at the time the question asks about, and adds the original dialogue text; a patient profile lists every recorded safety fact with every question. On MedMemoryBench, MedGraph has the highest overall accuracy with both a GPT and a Qwen backbone, 10.5 and 10.4 points above the strongest of five baselines, with efficient memory construction and answering in the GPT setting.
- **Key innovations:**
  - Diagnoses longitudinal dialogue memory failures (stale state, cross-person leakage, missing safety facts) and designs graph memory specifically to address them.
  - Builds dated, person-attributed fact chains (medication/diagnosis/measurement) with time-aware retrieval and persistent safety-fact profile for each query.
  - Achieves substantial gains on MedMemoryBench across GPT and Qwen backbones (+10.4–10.5 points over strongest baselines) with efficient construction/retrieval.

## 8. StepBack: Step-Level Error-Localized Resampling for Efficient Test-Time Reasoning
- **arXiv:** [2611.08403v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08403v1) (submitted 2026-11-05, cs.AI/cs.CL/cs.LG; NeurIPS 2026)
- **Authors:** Yang Ouyang, Jung-Eun Kim
- **Institution/Company:** Not specified on arXiv abstract page
- **Abstract:** Self-consistency (SC) is a widely used decoding strategy for improving chain-of-thought reasoning, but it requires sampling many complete reasoning trajectories with a preset budget. Its efficient variants reduce the cost mainly by stopping full-trajectory sampling early or distributing the number of samples across questions. In this paper, we propose StepBack, a step-level error-localized resampling method for efficient test-time reasoning. Given a small set of draft trajectories, StepBack localizes likely first error positions using lightweight signals and then resamples the detected error step and its suffix, followed by majority answer voting. Empirically, StepBack matches the accuracy of SC on average across seven mathematical reasoning and multi-hop QA benchmarks, while reducing token usage by up to 41.26%. In addition, by nature, StepBack can be combined with adaptive inference methods such as Adaptive Consistency, further improving their inference-time efficiency.
- **Key innovations:**
  - Moves beyond trajectory-level stopping/allocation by localizing likely first reasoning errors and resampling only from the error step onward.
  - Uses lightweight signals over draft trajectories to guide targeted suffix resampling plus majority voting.
  - Matches self-consistency accuracy on average across 7 math/multi-hop QA benchmarks with up to 41.26% token reduction and composes with adaptive inference methods.
