## §1 Game RL — Reinforcement Learning in Games

### ★★★ AdaMAP: Learning Adaptive Multi-Action Prediction with Grounded Dreaming Guidance
- **Authors**: Muyao Li, Zihao Wang, Xujing Li, Xiangyang Li, Jiabo Ye, Zhiyong Wu, Yaodong Yang, Yujia Qin
- **Affiliation**: not stated on the NeurIPS poster page (author group pages list Peking University / Tsinghua affiliations — ⚠️ **recorded as (tentative), not used in the analysis**).
- **Venue**: **NeurIPS 2026** (poster, Sydney site; announced 2026-09-25). **No arXiv version located** by title or keyword search.
- **Link**: https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/loc/sydney/poster/155834
- **Abstract and key innovations**: an **adaptive multi-action prediction** framework for game agents that (1) directly generates **variable-horizon action chunks** instead of fixed-length ones, (2) interleaves actions with **grounded visual dreams** — imagined frames that provide *verifiable future constraints* for the next chunk — and (3) optimizes the policy with **offline imitation learning followed by online MAP-PPO**. Evaluated on **Minecraft and VizDoom**: best or tied-best in **five of six task categories**, **+3.56 percentage points** average Minecraft success over the fixed-horizon RL baseline. The learned horizons **vary systematically across tasks and games**, which the authors read as evidence that adaptive multi-action prediction discovers useful **temporal abstractions** rather than merely longer outputs.
- **Why it matters here**: this is the **game-RL lane's first peer-reviewed NeurIPS-main result in the current digest series**, and its mechanism is the current frontier of the action-chunking/VLA line: the horizon itself becomes a learned control variable, and the "dreams" are constrained by verifiable futures rather than free hallucination — a direct answer to the world-model-daydreaming critique. The claim that learned horizons *systematize* across tasks is what separates it from a pure engineering variant.
- **Caveats**: poster, not oral; the +3.56 pp is one metric on one game suite; **no arXiv preprint means the wiki cannot link a stable full-text**, and the poster page carries no affiliation or code link.

### ★★ A Closed-Loop Non-Asymptotic Convergence Analysis of PPO with Learned Critics and Early Stopping
- **Authors**: Junwei Su, Mengfan Liu, Yanyong Zhang, Chuan Wu
- **Affiliation**: School of Artificial Intelligence and Data Science, **University of Science and Technology of China** (Su, Liu, Zhang, Wu) and Department of Computer Science, **The University of Hong Kong** (Wu). Recovered from the arXiv HTML authors block.
- **Venue**: arXiv:2610.10273 (7 Oct 2026) — `cs.LG`. **No venue acceptance claimed.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10273
- **Abstract and key innovations**: a **non-asymptotic analysis of PPO-Clip as a closed-loop actor–critic system**, explicitly modelling the four effects that prior analyses treat separately or not at all — **actor–critic coupling, nonsmooth probability-ratio clipping, finite-batch rollout reuse, and predictable early stopping** — under stated coverage and critic-regularity assumptions with raw GAE and Monte-Carlo critic targets. It gives **synchronous and asynchronous guarantees** jointly characterizing policy stationarity and critic tracking accuracy, and a **sufficient coupling condition under which optimization, critic tracking, clipping, and finite-batch errors share one amplification bound**. The asynchronous result extends the statement to the deployed-deployment pattern (stale/parallel rollouts).
- **Why it matters here**: PPO-Clip is the default optimizer behind essentially every self-play and game-agent training run in this wiki's corpus, and its failure modes (clip + reuse + learned critic interacting) are the standing folk explanation for unstable game training. This is the first analysis in the corpus that treats **the whole loop rather than one term**; the "common amplification bound" is the useful artifact — it says when the four error sources are *jointly* survivable.
- **Caveats**: **theory only — no experiments, no game environment run** (abstract reports no numbers). Assumptions are explicit but strong (coverage, critic regularity); "predictable early stopping" is a modelling choice, not a property of all PPO implementations. Preprint, unreviewed.

### ★★ Learning to Report Unsafe Tasks in a Multi-Agent Game
- **Authors**: Avyay M. Casheekar, Hariganesh Tangirala
- **Affiliation**: **University of Michigan Law School**, Ann Arbor (Casheekar) and School of Computing, **National University of Singapore** (Tangirala). Recovered from the arXiv HTML authors block. ⚠️ *Unusual pairing — a law-school first author on a PPO paper; reported as printed.*
- **Venue**: arXiv:2610.09002 (6 Oct 2026) — `cs.AI`, `cs.MA`. ACM CCS metadata present (multi-agent RL / cooperation / algorithmic game theory); **no venue acceptance claimed.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09002
- **Abstract and key innovations**: when agents share a reward, **reporting unsafe work costs the reporter the team's collective reward**, so silence can be the learned equilibrium even when audits make reporting the *optimal* policy — the paper's core distinction is between **an audit regime that makes universal reporting optimal** and **training that actually teaches a silent team to report**. It formalizes a witness game (any witness can stop a task by reporting), shows that with $k$ co-witnesses sharing a policy the expected-reward derivative counts each task's benefit $k$ times at universal silence (vs once under one-policy-per-role), derives an **audit condition sufficient — and, away from boundary cases, necessary near universal silence — for exact policy-gradient updates to reach universal reporting**, and shows the cheapest qualifying audits cost **exactly $k$× more under full policy sharing**. PPO experiments on **24 witness graphs**: **separating co-witnesses reduces unsafe completion by 33.59 percentage points**.
- **Why it matters here**: this is the cleanest game-theoretic statement in today's window of a **misaligned-incentive failure that RL gradient descent reliably finds** — the reward derivative is structurally wrong under shared policies, and no audit schedule fixes that without changing the policy grouping. The $k$× cost law and the 33.59 pp separation result are both quantitative and reusable for any multi-agent oversight design (RLHF annotator teams, agent swarms).
- **Caveats**: stylized witness game, not an established benchmark environment; the necessity direction of the audit condition excludes boundary cases; preprint, unreviewed, no venue.

### ★ Adapting to Changes in Agent Behavior via Finite-Depth Policy Sensitivity *(catch-up)*
- **Authors**: Lan Shi, Daigo Shishika, Xuan Wang
- **Affiliation**: **George Mason University**, USA (all three; printed in the HTML thanks block). Recovered from the arXiv HTML authors block.
- **Venue**: arXiv:2610.07475 (5 Oct 2026) — `cs.LG`. Comment: *"8 pages, 4 figures"*. **No venue acceptance claimed.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07475
- **Abstract and key innovations**: adapting an RL policy to **changes in another agent's behavior** normally needs large amounts of new interaction data; **policy sensitivity** — the first-order derivative of the optimal policy with respect to a behavioral parameter — would predict the adaptation, but exact computation needs **second-order derivatives propagated through future interactions**. The paper develops a **finite-depth framework** that truncates derivative propagation along the trajectory at an adjustable depth, approximating the policy Hessian and mixed derivative from a reference environment, derives **truncation-error bounds that are nonincreasing in depth and vanish at full horizon**, and validates the resulting sensitivity estimates in belief-state experiments.
- **Why it matters here**: opponent-parameter sensitivity is the theoretical object behind **fast adaptation to a changing adversary** — the recurring deployment problem in games (opponent shifts strategy mid-match). Making the Hessian propagation *truncatable with provable error* converts an offline curiosity into an online-usable quantity. Shishika's group also authored the 10-07 pursuit-evasion dominance paper — a continuing pursuit-evasion/theory thread in §1.
- **Caveats**: **theory + controlled experiments, no named game benchmark in the abstract**; requires a reference environment with the behavioral parameter present; below-window catch-up; preprint, unreviewed.

## §2 Game AI Bot — LLM-Powered Game Agents and NPC Intelligence

### ★★★ From Probabilities to Decisions: Search and Multi-Teacher Distillation with Jev
- **Authors**: Mohamad Yazan Sadoun, Sarah Sharif, Yaser Mike Banad
- **Affiliation**: INQUIRE Lab, School of Electrical and Computer Engineering, **University of Oklahoma**, Norman, OK, USA. Recovered from the arXiv HTML authors block.
- **Venue**: arXiv:2610.09188 (6 Oct 2026) — `cs.AI`. **No venue acceptance claimed.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09188
- **Abstract and key innovations**: studies **Jev**, a probability-only ("System One") model that returns calibrated probabilities for fixed choices and generates no text, on two decision-under-constraint tasks. **Bullet chess**: a bot that places Jev's pairwise judgment **inside Stockfish search** (with opening book and endgame tablebases) climbs **above a 2200 Lichess bullet rating against other bots**; live model calls are too slow for search, so judgments are **distilled into a compact evaluator run at every position**. **Multi-teacher budget allocation**: with Qwen3-32B available as a second labeling teacher under a fixed labeling budget, **averaging both judges' labels beats spending the whole budget on Qwen alone by 9.6 Elo (95% interval 4.3–14.9)**, and the gain replicates on fresh openings; **a second sample from the same judge is not a substitute for a second judge**. **Passage reranking**: Jev's labels alone train a reranker scoring as high as Qwen's from **21 minutes of API calls instead of 5.1 GPU-hours**, and adding Qwen gains at most thousandths in ranking quality.
- **Why it matters here**: this is the **strongest engineering result in today's arXiv window** and a rare *quantified* entry in the wiki's "small-model-as-judge" thread: the division of labor is stated crisply — **search supplies the lookahead, distillation makes judgment cheap enough for every position, and an LLM partner pays off only where judgment itself is the bottleneck (chess, not reranking)**. The "different teacher beats more samples of the same teacher" finding (+9.6 Elo) is a directly actionable budget-allocation rule for any distillation pipeline, and 2200+ bullet against bots is a number the game-agent lane can actually check.
- **Caveats**: preprint, unreviewed, no venue; the 2200 figure is **vs other bots on Lichess, not humans**; single model pair (Jev + Qwen3-32B); the reranking result shows the *opposite* cost structure, so the headline is task-dependent by the paper's own admission.

### ★★★ System Switch: When Should a Fast Decision Model Stop and Think?
- **Authors**: Gian Luca Bailo
- **Affiliation**: **Independent Researcher, Recco (Genoa), Italy** — self-declared in the HTML authors block (single author; **not inferred**).
- **Venue**: arXiv:2610.09683 (7 Oct 2026) — `cs.AI`. Comment: *"14 pages, 1 figure, 4 tables. Code, prompts and data: [URL]"*. **No venue acceptance claimed.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09683
- **Abstract and key innovations**: dual-process agents pair a fast policy with a slow deliberative model, but in real-time settings the slow model usually runs **continuously**; this paper inverts the duty cycle — **a fast learned actor takes every decision while the game keeps running, and hands control to a reasoning VLM only when a gate opens**. Evaluated on **closed-loop Doom** with the open "System One" typed-decision models served through a common llama.cpp interface, over **900 held-out questions**: (i) zero-shot decision models from **0.15B to 9B** choose to collect items **1.6–1.8× more often than chance among their errors**, in any option order (order shifts accuracy for some models); (ii) **accuracy, calibration, and sensitivity are distinct** — models of similar accuracy differ widely in AUROC, and the most sensitive model's confidence tracks *which kinds of situation* it fails, not which answers are wrong; (iii) **offline**, deferring the least-confident 30% of decisions gains over random deferral **in proportion to the actor's AUROC (rank correlation 0.87)** — with actor and rate chosen on held-out games the gain is **+0.13 [0.08, 0.18]** (doomLaya option order) and **+0.08 [0.02, 0.14]** (shuffled), with reasoning carrying about half of it; (iv) ⚠️ **in closed loop (33 games, three seeds) no variant reaches the exit** — committing to plans (the reasoner's or a fixed explore rule's) opens more doors and makes a stand-still actor play, but **with the rule the agent dies more often**, and told some doors need keys the reasoner takes ordinary paths.
- **Why it matters here**: this is the **latency-aware version of the escalate-to-VLM pattern** that the wiki's agent literature usually evaluates offline — here *the game keeps running while the gate decides*, which is the actual constraint in real-time bot deployment. The three-way separation of accuracy/calibration/sensitivity is a reusable evaluation point: **calibration alone does not identify when to defer; sensitivity does**; and the offline deferral gain tracking actor AUROC at ρ=0.87 makes *deferral value* a predictable function of actor quality. The closed-loop null (iv) is the day's starkest honesty: **every offline gain in the paper fails to produce a single exit in 33 games** — offline decision-accuracy improvements and closed-loop survival are shown to be different claims.
- **Caveats**: ⚠️ **the headline offline numbers do not survive closed loop** — no variant exits; single author, independent researcher — no institutional review context; single game domain (Doom), a 900-question offline harness plus 33 closed-loop games × 3 seeds; "System One" typed-decision models are a specific open family, so generality across VLMs is untested; preprint, unreviewed.

### ★ R-CNN-Based Chess Position Recognition
- **Authors**: Paras Govind, Ognjen Arandjelović
- **Affiliation**: School of Computer Science, **University of St Andrews**, United Kingdom. Recovered from the arXiv HTML authors block.
- **Venue**: arXiv:2610.09191 (6 Oct 2026) — `cs.CV`. **No venue acceptance claimed.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09191

## §3 Game Foundation Models & World Models for Games

### ★★★ Kuration SDK: Addressing the Virtual2Real Gap via Data Curation
- **Authors**: Nirmit Desai, Eric Song, Mayank Sengupta, Tejal Bedmutha, Siri Reddy, Sahiti Dharmavaram, Kunal Sawarkar
- **Affiliation**: **AIntropy AI** (all authors). Recovered from the arXiv HTML authors block.
- **Venue**: arXiv:2610.09305 (7 Oct 2026) — `cs.LG`, `cs.AI`, `cs.CV`, `cs.RO`. **No venue acceptance claimed.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09305
- **Abstract and key innovations**: benchmarks for action-conditioned world models are shifting from visual-similarity metrics toward action-semantic and physically-grounded ones, but for **domain/task-agnostic** training the existing metrics give weak signal. By training and evaluating **diffusion world models on CounterStrike gameplay data**, the paper confirms that **qualitative playability does not correspond with FVD, LPIPS, or JEDi** — and names this mismatch the **Virtual2Real gap**. Its argument: in the absence of reliable metrics, **curate the raw gameplay data and measure diagnostic properties *before* training**. The paper ships **Kuration SDK** (open-sourced with the paper) with several curation strategies, and reports that the SDK **found the root cause of a concrete Virtual2Real incident: two world models trained on identical map/action/state distributions behaved very differently when played despite very similar LPIPS and FVD scores**.
- **Why it matters here**: this is the **most direct attack on the metric stack this wiki's world-model lane has been implicitly using** — the corpus's world-model rankings (Matrix-Game, Solaris, WBench entries in prior digests) are built on exactly the FVD/LPIPS-family numbers this paper shows do not track playability. The double result — *metric fails* + *tool that diagnoses why* — is rarer than either half alone, and "two models, same scores, different behavior" is a falsification the whole evaluation literature in this wiki should respond to.
- **Caveats**: preprint, unreviewed, from a **small industry lab** (AIntropy AI) that both diagnoses the problem and sells the fix — read the SDK claim accordingly; the correspondence is demonstrated on **CounterStrike diffusion world models**, not across model families; "qualitative playability" is human-judged and the abstract reports no correlation coefficients.

### ★★ LeCuration: A Tiny World Model as a Data Curation Multi-Tool
- **Authors**: Mayank Sengupta, Nirmit Desai, Eric Song, Kunal Sawarkar
- **Affiliation**: **AIntropy AI** (all authors). Recovered from the arXiv HTML authors block.
- **Venue**: arXiv:2610.09285 (7 Oct 2026) — `cs.LG`, `cs.AI`, `cs.CV`, `cs.RO`. Comment: *"9 pages"*. **No venue acceptance claimed.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09285
- **Abstract and key innovations**: applications of physical AI — robots in warehouses, **agents moving around in video games** — run in closed physical worlds with few laws, so data for them should be curated *by the dataset's own physics*. **LeCuration** is a **small world model trained to serve as a curation tool for a separate larger downstream model**: it uses **LeWorldModel (LeWM) as latent encoder/predictor plus a diffusion-transformer (DiT) decoder** to add visuals to autoregressive gameplay rollouts, and its embeddings double as (1) an **anomaly-detection signal**, (2) a **content-based clustering heuristic**, and (3) via autoregressive state prediction, a **qualitative check of action–state consistency**. Presented as a **proof-of-concept case study on CS:GO gameplay data**, explicitly **without quantitative results yet**.
- **Why it matters here**: the inversion is the idea — **a world model used as a data-quality instrument rather than as a policy substrate or a generator**. For this wiki it is the companion piece to Kuration SDK (§3, same lab, same day): one names the metric failure, the other builds the cheap model that inspects the data behind it. Using a *tiny* model deliberately (curation doesn't need SOTA) is the right cost argument.
- **Caveats**: **qualitative, proof-of-concept, no quantitative evaluation by the paper's own admission**; same-lab, same-day pair with Kuration SDK — the two are **one research program, not independent corroboration**; industry preprint, unreviewed; CS:GO only.

### ★★★ Sparse Planning in Visual World Models via Cost Gradients (COSTGRAD)
- **Authors**: Yingchen Xu, Edward Grefenstette
- **Affiliation**: **University College London**. Recovered from the arXiv HTML authors block.
- **Venue**: arXiv:2610.10274 (7 Oct 2026) — `cs.LG`. Comment: **"Accepted at NeurIPS 2026. 20 pages, 6 figures, 8 tables. Project page and demos: [URL]"**.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10274
- **Abstract and key innovations**: token-based world models enable fine-grained latent planning, but repeatedly processing large spatial token grids makes action search expensive. **COSTGRAD** is a **training-free, goal-conditioned token selector** that ranks spatial tokens by the **gradient norm of the planning cost with respect to each input token** — importance is derived from the **downstream control objective**, not from prediction error. On AdaLN-conditioned predictors at **50% sparsity** it **matches or exceeds full-token planning on three of four continuous-control benchmarks** with a measured **2.6× wall-clock speedup per planning step**; combining token sparsity with reduced CEM search reaches **~5× total speedup while still exceeding the full-token baseline**. It also documents an **architecture-dependent failure mode**: in a matched comparison, concat-conditioned models keep full-token performance while pure COSTGRAD sparsity degrades them.
- **Why it matters here**: planning cost is *the* latency line for interactive world models, and this is the rare efficiency paper in the lane that **improves speed and holds or beats quality** rather than trading. The design principle — **rank by gradient through the objective you actually care about, not by predictor saliency** — transfers directly to game world-model controllers, and the honest architecture-dependent failure mode (AdaLN works, concat doesn't) is exactly the kind of boundary this wiki wants recorded before anyone generalizes the result.
- **Caveats**: NeurIPS 2026 accepted but **continuous-control benchmarks, not game environments** — the game relevance is by transfer; "training-free" assumes access to the cost gradient through the planner; failure mode means the headline 50% result is **architecture-conditional**.

## §7 Related Techniques — Self-Play, Game-Theoretic RL, and Interpretability

### ★★★ The Algorithm Is Not the Behavior: Learned Priors Override Look-Ahead in a Chess-Playing Neural Network
- **Authors**: Elias Sandmann, Sebastian Lapuschkin, Wojciech Samek
- **Affiliation**: **Fraunhofer HHI** (all three) and **TU Berlin** (Sandmann), **TU Dublin** (per the HTML affiliation spans — reported as printed). Recovered from the arXiv HTML authors block; contact `firstname.lastname@hhi.fraunhofer.de`.
- **Venue**: **NeurIPS 2026 — Oral** (Paris site; announced 2026-09-25). arXiv:2508.21380 v1 Aug 2025, **v3 10 Jun 2026** — **first wiki coverage**; the arXiv comment field carries no venue (the Oral status is from the NeurIPS site).
- **Link**: https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/loc/paris/poster/152268 · https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21380
- **Abstract and key innovations**: investigates **Leela Chess Zero**, the strongest neural chess engine, where prior work identified learned look-ahead. Extending the **logit lens** to its move-selecting policy network, the authors discover that correct puzzle solutions — **including immediate checkmates** — often appear in **intermediate layers but are systematically overridden in the final output**: a phenomenon they term **forgotten puzzles**. Replicating prior look-ahead analyses on these positions shows **look-ahead operates normally** — future moves of the correct continuation are represented, causally important, and linearly decodable — **ruling out an algorithm failure**. Instead, **late layers increasingly prioritize safe play over aggression**; **steering the model against these preferences recovers 61.7% of forgotten puzzles**, causal evidence that **safety priors override algorithmically computed solutions**. The stated conclusion: **algorithmic structure does not guarantee algorithmic behavior — a model can internally solve a problem and still output the wrong answer.**
- **Why it matters here**: this is the **strongest mechanistic result about a game-playing network in the wiki** and the sharpest version yet of its recurring theme — *internal competence ≠ reported/acted competence* (10-05's observation-belief gap, 10-05's alignment-without-partner-specificity, today's own §8). The **61.7% causal recovery via steering** elevates it from descriptive logit-lens storytelling to an intervention, and the safety-vs-aggression reading gives a *named mechanism* for the "plays the safe losing move" failure every chess-bot evaluator has seen. A NeurIPS **Oral** — the highest-visibility venue item in this run.
- **Caveats**: arXiv v1 predates the window by a year (this is a **proceedings-channel find, not a fresh preprint**); single network family (Leela Chess Zero); "safety prior" is inferred from layer-wise tendencies plus steering — the steering result is strong but the *interpretation* of what the late layers encode remains model-level, not human-validated.

### ★★ Sharper Bounds on the Thresholds for Sensitive and Blackwell Optimality
- **Authors**: Xavier Allamigeon, Stéphane Gaubert, Julien Grand-Clément, Ricardo Katz
- **Affiliation**: **INRIA and CMAP, École Polytechnique** (Allamigeon, Gaubert, Grand-Clément), **ISOM Department, HEC Paris** (Katz), **CIFASIS-CONICET** (Katz). Recovered from the arXiv HTML authors block.
- **Venue**: arXiv:2610.10295 (7 Oct 2026) — `cs.GT`. **No venue acceptance claimed.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10295
- **Abstract and key innovations**: in perfect-information two-player **stochastic games**, Blackwell and $d$-sensitive optimality generalize mean-payoff and discount criteria to farsighted preferences; the **Blackwell threshold** $\alpha_{\sf bw}$ is the smallest discount factor above which discount-optimal and Blackwell-optimal policies coincide. The paper bounds these thresholds by focusing on **reduced families of polynomials**, and its bounds are **tight in controlling the degrees and heights of the minimal polynomials** of the thresholds. Applying classical **root separation (Mahler's and Cauchy's bounds)** to the reduced families yields **the strongest upper and lower bounds on $\alpha_{\sf bw}$ in the literature**, and the **first bounds on $\alpha_{\sf d}$ in the multichain stochastic setting**.
- **Why it matters here**: the Blackwell threshold answers a practical question for game RL — **how far into the future must a policy look before discount-optimal play is provably enough?** — and the wiki's stochastic-game theory entries (10-07's bandit-feedback Markov games, today's §1 PPO analysis) all quietly assume a discounting regime without bounding where it is safe to do so. Tight degree/height control is what makes the bound *computable* rather than asymptotic, and the multichain extension closes the case that covers games with multiple recurrent classes — i.e. most real game environments.
- **Caveats**: **pure theory — no experiments, no algorithms run**; perfect-information setting (the wiki's imperfect-information/poker lane is out of scope); preprint, unreviewed; "strongest in the literature" is the authors' claim against prior work they cite.

### ★★ Beyond Nominal Equilibria: Risk-Averse Multi-Population Mean-Field Games
- **Authors**: Bhavini Jeloka, Siddhartha Ganguly, Panagiotis Tsiotras (equal contribution noted in the HTML)
- **Affiliation**: Daniel Guggenheim School of Aerospace Engineering, **Georgia Institute of Technology**, Atlanta, USA (all three; from the HTML thanks block).
- **Venue**: arXiv:2610.09244 (7 Oct 2026) — `math.OC`, `cs.GT`, `cs.LG`, `eess.SY`. Comment: *"Submitted to a conference; comments are welcome"*. **No venue acceptance claimed.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09244
- **Abstract and key innovations**: multi-population mean-field games model large-scale heterogeneous agent systems via representative agents and their mean-field distributions, but existing approaches **do not account for uncertainty in other populations' behavior**. This paper introduces **risk-averse multi-population MFGs**, where each population optimizes a **worst-case expected reward over dynamically feasible ambiguity sets of the mean-field flows of a subset of the other populations**. Using an **occupation-measure formulation** and set-valued analysis, it establishes geometric properties of the ambiguity sets and **existence of a risk-averse multi-population mean-field equilibrium**, derives **contractivity of the fixed-point operator under entropy regularization** (making the equilibrium learnable), and proposes a **risk-averse fictitious-play** algorithm.
- **Why it matters here**: mean-field methods are the wiki's standing answer to **many-agent games** (hundreds of NPCs, crowd populations), and the standard formulation's assumption — *you know the other population's distribution* — is exactly what fails in open multiplayer settings. Putting **ambiguity sets over other populations' flows** converts "model the swarm" into "model the swarm *worst-case*", and entropy-regularized contractivity is what keeps the equilibrium **learnable by the fictitious-play machinery the corpus already uses**. Tsiotras's group continues its active game-theory thread (10-07 pursuit-evasion dominance, §1 catch-up 07475 shares the GMU/Georgia Tech orbit).
- **Caveats**: theory (existence + contractivity + algorithm sketch); the ambiguity-set choice is a modelling decision the abstract does not justify empirically; no experiments quoted; preprint, unreviewed.
