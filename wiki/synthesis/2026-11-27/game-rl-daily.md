### 2611.34536v1 — "Kant-Q: A Q-Learning Algorithm for Kantian Equilibrium in Symmetric Markov Games" (2026-11-25)
- **Authors**: Rishi Bharadwaj; Sujit Gujar; Yadati Narahari
- **Affiliation**: **Rishi Bharadwaj** & **Yadati Narahari**: Department of Computer Science and Automation, **Indian Institute of Science (IISc), Bengaluru, India**; **Sujit Gujar**: **International Institute of Information Technology (IIIT), Hyderabad, India**
- **Venue/conference**: arXiv preprint (2026-11-25); cs.LG/cs.GT; 12 pages, 3 figures
- **Abstract**: AI agents increasingly interact with one another, and many of these interactions are mixed-motive games, which have posed a persistent challenge for learning algorithms. Most multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) algorithms are designed to reach Nash equilibria, yet in many such games these equilibria are Pareto-inefficient. We instead study Kantian equilibria, an economic solution concept in which each agent plays the action it would want all agents to adopt. In symmetric games, this corresponds to an optimal common policy. We propose Kant-Q, a Q-learning algorithm in which each agent learns action profiles where all agents play the same action. Each update is then a sample from a single-agent MDP, so Kant-Q inherits the convergence guarantees of standard Q-learning. We show that every symmetric Markov game has a pure Kantian equilibrium. We also prove that all agents' Q-tables converge to the same limit, whose greedy policy is such an equilibrium, and that greedy play converges whenever the optimal common action is unique at every state. We further show that in a subclass of symmetric monotone games, the Kantian equilibrium is Pareto-efficient among pure strategy profiles and strictly individually rational. Empirically, on the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma and the GovSim fishery with up to 200 agents, Kant-Q learns the Kantian equilibrium, while the baselines underperform. Since copies of the same AI system naturally face one another in symmetric games, these results suggest universalization as a design principle for cooperative AI-AI interaction.
- **Key innovations**: **Kantian equilibrium** in symmetric Markov games; **Kant-Q** Q-learning for common-policy profiles; update reduces to a single-agent MDP sample; proofs: pure Kantian equilibrium exists; Q-tables converge; greedy play converges; Pareto-efficiency + strict individual rationality in symmetric monotone games; empirical iterated Prisoner's Dilemma + **GovSim** fishery up to 200 agents; **universalization** as a cooperative-AI design principle. Not a classic video-game policy paper.

- **Authors**: Solaleh Mohammadi; Yiman Fong; Kaiqing Zhang

- **Abstract**: We study evolutionary learning in coordination games in a finite population of agents with heterogeneous risk attitudes, modeled using the entropic risk measure (ERM). We show that a sufficiently large intervention share (the fraction of agents modified) and sufficiently high intervention intensity (the increase in their risk parameter) ensure that the payoff-dominant equilibrium is uniquely stochastically stable. - **Affiliation**: **IIIT-Delhi** (Indraprastha Institute of Information Technology Delhi), New Delhi, India

- **Abstract**: In multi-agent interaction, a learner's actions shape the observations from which it infers other agents' behavior. ### 2611.28351v1 — "Coupling-Aware Reinforcement Learning for Co-Evolving Graph Games" (2026-11-20)

- **Affiliation**: H. Milton Stewart School of Industrial and Systems Engineering, **Georgia Institute of Technology**, Atlanta, GA 30332, USA

- **Abstract**: Building hydrogen station networks, electric vehicle charging grids, or vaccine distribution systems requires coordinating two separately operated graphs, demand-side facilities and the supply-side networks they depend on, where each operator's payoff depends on the other's choices. ### 2611.25981v1 — "Safe Subgame Resolving in Multiplayer Games" (2026-11-19)
- **Authors**: Boning Li; Longbo Huang
- **Affiliation**: **Institute for Interdisciplinary Information Sciences (IIIS), Tsinghua University**, Beijing, China
- **Venue/conference**: arXiv preprint (2026-11-19; paper dated November 20, 2026 on PDF); cs.GT/cs.AI; 44 pages; code https://github.com/lbn187/MultiPlayerSolver
- **Abstract**: Poker and other games with hidden information are too large to solve in one pass. Strong systems compute a complete blueprint strategy offline and refine it in real time inside the reached subgame. In 2p0s games, a safety theorem guarantees that subgame resolving never increases how much an adversary can gain by exploiting the resolved strategy relative to the blueprint strategy. With three or more players acting independently, no such guarantee exists yet, and heuristic resolving can increase the profile's best-response gap. We prove the first conditional safety theorem for uncorrelated multiplayer subgame resolving, a post-hoc certificate for any delivered update. The best-response gap of the resolved profile exceeds that of the blueprint by at most four terms. Two concern the resolver: the subgame value the blueprint promised without realizing (A_i^S) and the resolver's subgame solve error (ε_i). The other two are the opponents' best-response gains inside the subgame over their promised values (Σ_q M_q) and a structural tax (Θ), which measures how much the players' total utility can change. The tax is zero in constant-sum games, including every rake-free poker variant. Constructions attain each term with equality. On three-player Leduc poker and on Kuhn poker with up to six players, resolving without a certificate raises the gap beyond a small tolerance in 12% to 100% of the updates. The certificate flags every such update and accepts 41% (Kuhn) and 86% (Leduc) of the standard resolving updates. A resolver that keeps the blueprint whenever the certificate exceeds the tolerance delivers no harmful update. The certificate needs only one best-response pass per opponent inside the subgame, so real-time multiplayer solvers that refine a blueprint can attach it as a safety check. Code is available at https://github.com/lbn187/MultiPlayerSolver
- **Key innovations**: first **conditional safety theorem for uncorrelated multiplayer subgame resolving**; **post-hoc safety certificate**; best-response-gap bound decomposed into four terms (A_i^S, ε_i, Σ_q M_q, Θ); structural tax Θ; zero tax in constant-sum incl. rake-free poker; one best-response pass per opponent; real-time blueprint refinement; evaluated on 3-player Leduc + Kuhn up to 6 players; **poker**; safe fallback to blueprint. (Poker metrics author-reported.)

### 2611.30006v1 — "Sequential Selection against an Adaptive Opponent" (2026-11-23)
- **Authors**: Zhongyuan Lyu; Xiaocong Xu
- **Affiliation**: **Zhongyuan Lyu**: The University of Sydney Business School, **University of Sydney**, Australia; **Xiaocong Xu**: Department of Statistics, **University of Pittsburgh**, Pittsburgh, PA, USA
- **Venue/conference**: arXiv preprint (2026-11-23); stat.ML/cs.LG/math.ST/stat.ME; 50 pages; **no conference named**
- **Abstract**: Selecting a strategy by testing it against an opponent is common in game-playing AI, red-teaming of language models, and safety evaluations of AI agents. In many of these settings, the opponent adapts to what it observes, so a strategy that keeps winning in practice may already have stopped working by the time it is used. Motivated by these settings, we study a player who tries strategies in practice games against an adaptive opponent, decides when to stop, and then either selects one strategy for the next game or abstains. The goal is to select a strategy that meets a target win probability while rarely selecting one that has already fallen below it. For a single strategy, we propose the Sequential Private Accumulated-excess Rule (SPAR), which accumulates evidence through a capped likelihood ratio and selects the strategy once this evidence reaches a privately drawn uniform threshold. We show that the stopping time must involve such private randomness. The number of games SPAR needs is inversely proportional to the error tolerance, and this rate is optimal even when the opponent happens not to adapt. To select among multiple strategies, we introduce Screen-then-SPAR, which screens candidates in stages before applying SPAR. The procedure adapts to the best strategy's unknown initial margin above the target and achieves the minimax rate up to logarithmic factors. The performance of Screen-then-SPAR is demonstrated through simulations and two real-data studies, one using recorded coding-agent attacks from MonitoringBench and the other using rock-paper-scissors matches between human players and an adaptive bot.
- **Key innovations**: **sequential strategy selection against an adaptive opponent**; **Sequential Private Accumulated-excess Rule (SPAR)**; capped likelihood ratio; private random stopping threshold; private randomness necessary; inverse error-tolerance sample rate; optimality; **Screen-then-SPAR** multi-strategy screening; minimax rate up to logs; applied to game-playing AI; **rock-paper-scissors human-vs-adaptive-bot** real-data; also LLM red-teaming / safety eval framing.

### 2611.26084v1 — "RL Can Only Reinforce What It Sees Succeed" (2026-11-19)
- **Authors**: Ruben Sanchez-Corcuera; Unai Zulaika; Aritz Bilbao-Jayo; Miguel Fernandez-de-Retana
- **Affiliation**: **University of Deusto**, Bilbao, Spain
- **Venue/conference**: arXiv preprint (2026-11-19); cs.LG/cs.CL; 16 pages, 6 figures, 1 table; **no conference named**
- **Abstract**: When a familiar rule stops working, an agent has to weigh what pre-training taught it against what it now observes. We ask how outcome-reward reinforcement learning (RL) changes this balance, in a puzzle world where both sides are known: small language models trained from scratch on games played by a Bayesian player, so that the prior and the right answer are exact. Base models hedge as their corpus did, and RL turns that hedging into decisions, in whichever direction the reward favours. But RL can only reinforce what it sees succeed. About 35 such groups give even odds. The count holds for new and repeated problems and for groups of 4 to 256 attempts, and across learning rates, optimizers, GRPO variants and model sizes from 1.3M to 85M parameters, which move the threshold but not what is counted.
- **Key innovations**: outcome-reward RL in a **puzzle world with exact Bayesian-player games**; small LMs trained from scratch; **"RL can only reinforce what it sees succeed"**; rare-correct-action phase transition; **expected number of winning attempt-groups**; ~35 groups for even odds; robustness across group sizes 4–256, learning rates, optimizers, GRPO variants, model sizes 1.3M–85M; pre-training prior vs new evidence; not a classic video-game benchmark.
