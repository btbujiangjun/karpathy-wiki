### 2611.34035v1 — "Post-Training Policy Replication for Strategy-Following Multi-Agent Deep Reinforcement Learning across Team Sizes" (2026-11-25)
- **Authors**: Yamato Takahagi; Yoshinari Motokawa; Toshiharu Sugawara
- **Affiliation**: Department of Computer Science and Communications Engineering, Waseda University, Tokyo, Japan
- **Venue/conference**: Accepted to the **19th International Conference on Agents and Artificial Intelligence (ICAART 2027)**, INSTICC, Feb 2027, Valletta, Malta; arXiv preprint (2026-11-25); cs.MA/cs.AI/cs.LG
- **Abstract**: Although multi-agent deep reinforcement learning enables coordinated behavior, aligning it with managerial intent remains difficult due to complex agent interactions. Although the strategy-following distributed attentional actor architecture (sfDA7-X) improves controllability via regional instructions, instruction combinations scale exponentially with team and instruction sizes, leading to prohibitive training costs and poor transferability. To reduce this computational cost, we introduce a team-size-independent observation architecture and reconstruct collective behavior by replicating a single trained policy across agents in teams of different sizes, thereby eliminating the need to train from scratch. Additionally, we evaluate warm-start retraining by initializing policy networks with a pre-trained policy to significantly lower computational overhead. In an object collection game, a policy trained in a four-agent team is deployed to teams of three to eight agents. Experiments demonstrate that training with the proposed aggregated observation matrix achieves performance comparable to that of the baseline method, sfDA7-X. Furthermore, policy replication preserves performance without substantial degradation in three- and five-agent teams, and warm-start retraining outperforms training from scratch while reducing required training episodes by up to 90%. Deployments to six- and eight-agent teams further confirm effective spatial region allocation following given instructions. These results demonstrate that the proposed method enables zero-shot collective behavior reconstruction via policy replication.
- **Key innovations**: sfDA7-X strategy-following distributed attentional actor; **team-size-independent aggregated observation matrix**; **policy replication** (one trained policy reused across team sizes, no retraining); **warm-start retraining** (up to 90% fewer episodes); zero-shot collective-behavior reconstruction in an object-collection game; trained at 4 agents → deployed at 3–8 agents.
- **Links**: [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34035v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.34035v1)

### 2611.32812v1 — "Breaking the Joint Action Space Dependence in Distributionally Robust Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning" (2026-11-24)
- **Authors**: Yue Wang
- **Affiliation**: University of Central Florida
- **Venue/conference**: arXiv preprint (2026-11-24), "work in progress"; cs.LG
- **Abstract**: We study the sample complexity of learning robust coarse correlated equilibria (CCE) in finite-horizon, general-sum, distributionally robust Markov games with known rewards and a generative model of the nominal transitions. Each player evaluates a policy by its worst-case return over total-variation (TV) balls centered at the nominal transition distribution of every stage, state, and joint action; this is the standard (s,a)-rectangular uncertainty set. Existing generative-model guarantees for this model scale with the number ∏ᵢAᵢ of joint actions, where Aᵢ is the number of actions of player i. Since this number grows exponentially with the number of players, these guarantees suffer from the curse of multiagency. We design an algorithm whose sample complexity depends on the action sets only through the largest individual action-set size A* := maxᵢAᵢ. The algorithm uses Õ(SA*H⁵D⁴/ε⁴) transition samples and, with probability at least 1−δ, returns a Markov policy that is an ε-robust CCE from every initial state. Here S is the number of states, H is the horizon, and D := maxᵢ min{H,1/ρᵢ}, where ρᵢ is the TV radius of player i. Two ingredients make this possible: a plug-in estimate of the robust backup whose mean error does not depend on the number of states, and a single batch of samples per round that serves as bandit feedback for all players at once. The result shows that the dependence on ∏ᵢAᵢ can be avoided without modifying the (s,a)-rectangular uncertainty set.
- **Key innovations**: **distributionally robust MARL** (worst-case TV-ball transitions, (s,a)-rectangular); **breaks exponential joint-action-space dependence** (∏ᵢAᵢ → A*); robust-CCE sample complexity Õ(SA*H⁵D⁴/ε⁴); shared single batch serving all players; combats sim-to-real gap in multi-agent settings.
- **Links**: [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32812v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.32812v1)

### 2611.32031v1 — "Unifying Partner and Environment Diversity to Improve Human-AI Coordination" (2026-11-24)
- **Authors**: Patrick Q. Zhang; Yancheng Liang; Adrienne Fairhall; Natasha Jaques
- **Affiliation**: Not explicitly stated on PDF p.1; author e-mails indicate **University of Washington** (@uw.edu / @cs.washington.edu). Natasha Jaques is a UW faculty member (not stated in-paper).
- **Venue/conference**: **NeurIPS 2026**; arXiv preprint (2026-11-24); cs.MA/cs.AI/cs.LG
- **Abstract**: Real-world applications of modern AI systems require generalization to both unseen environments and novel human users. The field of zero-shot coordination (ZSC) has focused primarily on deploying AI to cooperate with novel partners in otherwise familiar environments. Popular population-based methods achieve this by partnering the agent with a diversity of simulated human users during training in a fixed environment, but does not consider the more realistic scenario where both the users and the environment are unseen during training. Recently, training across environments has been explored in training a single self-play agent, which shows better generalization on both novel human users and unseen environments. In this work, we ask whether jointly modeling partner diversity and environment diversity yields stronger coordination with novel partners in novel environments. We introduce Cross Environment Co-Play (CECP), a two-stage framework that first trains a population of self-play agents across a distribution of procedurally generated environments, and then trains a cooperator policy against this partner population. To further couple social and environmental reasoning, we add a next-state prediction module that encourages representations to encode both partner behavior and task structure. We evaluate CECP on both a neuroscience-inspired cooperative foraging task, and the popular ZSC benchmark Overcooked, under held-out partner and held-out environment conditions. CECP achieves the strongest performance among the compared baselines and attains state-of-the-art results under our evaluation protocol for human-AI collaboration. We analyze the model's latent representation encoded by the next-state prediction module, showing it can not only improve the performance of the cooperative AI, but also provide more interpretable latents, demonstrated by the inference of partner skill levels.
- **Key innovations**: zero-shot coordination with **both** novel partners *and* novel environments; **Cross Environment Co-Play (CECP)**; two-stage **population-based self-play across procedurally generated environments** + cooperator training; **next-state prediction** auxiliary; SOTA on Overcooked + cooperative foraging; interpretable partner-skill latents.

- **Abstract**: Social-deduction games give models opposing objectives, but they also give roles different information and speaking obligations. We analyze 5,404 public utterances from 150 Chinese Werewolf games played by 12 models, comparing ordinary wolves with ordinary villagers so that special roles are excluded. ### 2611.34152v1 — "Lost in the Grid: Where Multimodal LLMs Fail Between Seeing and Planning" (GridProbe) (2026-11-25)
- **Authors**: Luoxi Zhang¹*; Tianyi Tan²*; Jiawei Liu¹*; Xingyu Zeng¹; Min Yang³; Shiwen Ni¹† (*equal contribution; †corresponding)
- **Affiliation**: ¹Shenzhen University of Advanced Technology; ²Dalian University of Technology; ³Shenzhen Institutes of Advanced Technology
- **Venue/conference**: arXiv preprint (2026-11-25); cs.CV/cs.AI; 31 pages; code https://github.com/suat-llm/GridProbe
- **Abstract**: Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) excel at static visual understanding and are increasingly applied to spatial planning, where a model interprets a visual layout and outputs an action sequence to reach a goal. Grid games are a common testbed for this ability because their rules are explicit and a solver can verify every move. Yet existing benchmarks mostly report end-to-end success rates, which hide whether a failure stems from visual perception, rule application, or multi-step planning. We introduce GridProbe, a diagnostic benchmark that 1) locates the failing stage between visual perception and planning and 2) verifies the diagnosis by targeted fine-tuning. On Sokoban, GridProbe pairs end-to-end solving with four probes (recognition, localization, single-step prediction, and state tracking) and gives every board both as an image and as an equivalent text grid. Evaluating seven MLLMs on Sokoban and a maze game reveals distinct bottlenecks behind similar success rates: proprietary models are limited by multi-step planning, whereas open-source models are limited by rule application or by visual grid indexing, i.e., counting rows and columns. Planning failures also hide perception failures: when planning is easier, the visual cost of the same model grows from 7.6 to 37.7 points. The diagnosis identifies what to train: fine-tuning only the vision module of the perception-limited model on board reading raises its interactive Sokoban success 3.5-fold (5.8% to 20.4%) and transfers zero-shot to the unseen maze game (single-turn success 13.8% to 67.1%), whereas the same data does not help a rule-limited model and training on solver solutions brings no gain.
- **Key innovations**: **diagnostic stage localization** (perception vs rule application vs multi-step planning); four probes (recognition, localization, single-step prediction, state tracking); image+text grid; **targeted fine-tuning verification**; proprietary-vs-open-source failure-mode split; "planning failures hide perception failures"; vision-module fine-tuning result.
- **Links**: [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34152v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.34152v1) · [code](https://github.com/suat-llm/GridProbe)

### 2611.33850v1 — "Mitigating Overgeneralization in RND via Spectral Target Design" (2026-11-25)
- **Authors**: Minseok Jeong¹*; Yechan Lee¹*; Hyewon Choi¹; Jeongyong Yang²; SooJean Han¹† (*equal contribution; †corresponding)
- **Affiliation**: ¹KAIST; ²University of Washington

- **Abstract**: Random Network Distillation (RND) is a scalable novelty signal for RL based on a fixed random target network and a trained predictor. - **Key innovations**: kernel-theoretic analysis of **RND overgeneralization**; novelty = **cross-kernel residual variance**; **spectral under-excitation** diagnosis; **spectral target design**; **bandwidth-controlled Random Fourier Feature (RFF) target**; gains on offline D4RL + **online Atari** + Fetch manipulation; **curiosity-driven exploration** rigor.

### 2611.29752v1 — "Why Units Die in the Layer After a Frozen Random Encoder, and What the Number of Live Units Measures" (2026-11-22)
- **Authors**: Scott M. Norton
- **Affiliation**: Independent Researcher
- **Venue/conference**: arXiv preprint (2026-11-22); cs.LG; 31 pages + 121-page ancillary supplement
- **Abstract**: Reinforcement-learning agents can learn Atari games through a convolutional encoder whose random weights are never trained. They reach expert play in Pong and, without random action repeats, in Breakout, but training takes far longer and few ReLU units in the layer after the encoder stay alive. A dead unit outputs zero for every input and cannot learn. In earlier work we took the number of live units to measure the game's complexity. Here we ask why units die and correct that conclusion. The evidence is from small networks and three games.
- **Key innovations**: explains **dead ReLU units after a frozen random encoder** (RND-style Atari); defines **"offset"**; predictive formula; **mean-subtraction** practical fix; corrects earlier "live-units measures complexity" claim; honestly scoped (small nets, 3 games); **Atari Pong/Breakout**.
