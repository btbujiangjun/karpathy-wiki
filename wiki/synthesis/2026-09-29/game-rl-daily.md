## §0.1 Why This Digest Exists: A Method Failure, Not a Drought

> **The negative finding in Part I is real but it is not the interesting one.** The interesting one is what the retrospective sweep exposes about the *daily-digest protocol itself*.

### 0.1.1 Five consecutive "empty" game windows were a sampling artifact of the method
The 09-25, 09-28 and today digests all report a game section that is thin or empty, and prior reports read that as *the field moving elsewhere*. The topic sweep says otherwise. Searching the same 7.5-month archive the daily digests have been sampling from, with the *same* corpus and the *same* hard game tokens, produces **43 game-domain papers the wiki has never claimed** — including a **production-deployed Unity tooling paper** (2609.27585), a **70-task Unreal Engine benchmark** (2609.23142), an **unsound-search card-game result that beats a champion bot** (2609.06816), and a **PCG line going back to 2025-09** that the daily digests have been declaring "vacant" window after window.
- **Mechanism**: at ~1,260 papers per window and a game-paper rate of roughly 1–2 per *multi-window* period in cs.AI/cs.LG/cs.RO, a 24-hour sample has an expected game yield near zero. A topic query over 7.5 months has ~200× the sample.
- **The specific blind spot**: window digests sweep `cs.AI / cs.LG / cs.CL / cs.RO / cs.CV`. **Game PCG lives in `cs.DS`, `cs.GR`, `cs.CY`, and `cs.HC`; game-engine tooling lives in `cs.SE`; game economy and anti-cheat live in `cs.CR`; game HCI lives in `cs.HC`.** The 09-28 report flagged `cs.DS` as "worth a dedicated keyword sweep" — this run does that sweep, and it is where the 2025-09 → 2026-06 PCG block (§4) comes from.
- **Standing rule proposed for future runs:** a game digest should carry **both** a fresh-window pass (for latency on new work) **and** a rolling topic sweep at ~quarterly cadence (for coverage). A window pass alone structurally under-reports the field.

### 0.1.2 The game line is not shrinking — it is *moving out of the RL-conference categories*
Every featured paper in §1–§7 that touches a real game environment does so in one of three registers, and the distribution has shifted:
1. **Game-as-testbed** (dominant): a real game is used to measure a general capability (belief calibration 2608.24691, geometric inductive bias 2608.14982, harness-weight co-adaptation 2609.00196, language-invariance of skill 2608.25832).
2. **Engine-as-workload** (new since ~2026-08): the object of study is the *engine repository* and the agent working inside it (2609.23142 CraftBench-UE, 2609.27585 Unity Insight, 2608.27797 CEDAR-in-Minecraft). This register barely existed in earlier digests.
3. **Player-as-evaluator** (rising): the outcome measure is a human player's experience, not a score (2609.13727, 2609.13718, 2608.28729, 2605.30864).
The register that has *not* grown is "new RL algorithm beats baseline on Atari." That specific line is genuinely quiet, and §1 says so explicitly rather than padding the section with adjacent robotics.

## §1 Game RL: Imperfect Information, Self-Play, and the Limits of Representation

> Five papers. The section's through-line is a **consistent gap between what a representation learns and what the closed loop does with it** — stated honestly by three of the five authors.

### 1.1 Unsound Search with Policy and Value Networks in Legends of Code and Magic
- **Authors**: Dustin Rubin
- **Affiliation**: not stated (single author; associated with the ByteRL / Netrunner community) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.AI, 2026-09-06** (6 pages, 3 figures, 1 table; also a Zenodo preprint, doi:10.5281/zenodo.22547763)
- **Abstract and key innovations**: Collectible card games are imperfect-information games with large belief states; **Legends of Code and Magic (LoCM)** has belief states of size **2^101**. The reigning champion, **ByteRL, plays with no search at all**, and prior work claims sound enumeration-based search is unusable in the genre because of the belief-state count. The paper takes the opposing position. It measures **three previously-defined properties that predict where theoretically unsound perfect-information MCTS's defects are cheap**, and finds LoCM sits in the favorable region. The agent starts from **imitation learning of the runner-up policy (NeteaseOPD)** to produce a policy-and-value feed-forward network, then **searches over worlds sampled from a prior over the opponent's deck, built from the runner-up's own drafts**.
- **Results**: The strictest configuration, battle phase, **beats ByteRL at 51.35% win rate, 95% CI [50.37, 52.33], over 10,000 pre-registered games** using the official LoCM referee and time limit. **Search is not a minor factor on this matchup**: without search the same agent scores 26.8%, so search adds **+24.6 points**. The paper then **replicates a published best-response attack against ByteRL** and applies the identical protocol to two configurations of its own agent — **each resists the attack better than ByteRL at every iteration**.
- **Significance**: The strongest single result in this digest, and it is strong in the way that matters: it overturns a published impossibility claim with a **pre-registered, refereed, 10,000-game head-to-head**, not a sweep metric. Two transferable lessons. **(1) The standard argument against search in imperfect-information games — "the belief space is too big" — is answered by the structure of the specific game, not by search in general**; the three predictability properties are the reusable diagnostic, and they should be computed before declaring a game unsuited to search. **(2) Unsoundness is not automatically an exploitable liability.** The exploit probe is the part to copy: build the best-response attack, run it against *both* the incumbent and your own system, and report the result either way. The honest framing the paper keeps — "could be exploitable" — is the right register.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.06816

### 1.2 Do Geometry-Aware Positional Encodings Help Transformers in Spatial Imperfect-Information Games?
- **Authors**: Wenji Fu
- **Affiliation**: not stated (single author) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.LG/cs.AI/stat.ML, 2026-08-15** (7 pages, 4 figures, 3 tables)
- **Abstract and key innovations**: Transformers in spatial imperfect-information games must represent **map geometry** while tracking **hidden entities through time**. The paper asks whether geometry-aware positional encodings help, and states up front that it does **not** claim a new positional encoding. **HexRoPE** (a hex-grid rotary encoding) is benchmarked on a four-level suite over a **hexagonal naval-pursuit game**: controlled geometry and topology probes; an **exact-Bayes hidden-target tracking task**; **offline policy imitation at 1k and 10k games**; and **7,200 fixed-seed games against three legacy opponents**.
- **Results**: On belief quality, HexRoPE reduces exact-belief posterior cross-entropy vs no encoding by **0.278 on D6-transformed test orbits and 0.329 on a larger map**; both hierarchical-bootstrap CIs exclude zero, both Holm-adjusted p < 0.001. On policy imitation at 1k games: **+4.63 pp** action accuracy over no encoding, **+2.05 pp** over rectangular relative bias — **but the gains shrink to 1.55 and 0.41 pp at 10k games**. **And the headline: HexRoPE does not improve aggregate gameplay win rate at all** — paired effect over no encoding is **−1.56 pp, 95% CI [−4.50, 1.17]**. Separately, rectangular relative bias is strongest on belief consistency but **fails sharply extrapolating from radius 3 to radius 4**; graph bias gives only a small blocked-edge gain.
- **Significance**: A **four-level benchmark design worth copying** — controlled probe → exact-Bayes task → imitation at two data scales → closed-loop play, with the scales chosen so the data-efficiency effect is visible before the closed-loop effect washes out. The result is a clean instance of the **representation-does-not-transfer-to-behavior** gap: the encoding measurably improves the intermediate quantity it was designed to improve (belief calibration) and the win rate does not move. Two secondary findings matter as much: the **extrapolation failure from radius 3→4** is a cheap, reproducible test of whether a positional encoding has actually learned the geometry or memorized the training radius; and the **shrinkage from 1k to 10k** says the imitation gain is a low-data artifact, not a capability the encoding adds. For game agents the actionable reading is negative: **before adopting a geometry-aware encoding, check whether the win rate moved.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14982

### 1.3 JaxAHT: A JAX-Based Library for Ad Hoc Teamwork
- **Authors**: Caroline Wang, Rolando Fernandez, Zelal Su Mustafaoglu, Montek Kundan, Jiaxun Cui, Lingyun Xiao, Zhihan Wang, Di Yang Shi, Aditya Madhan, Johnny Liu, Arrasy Rahman, Peter Stone
- **Affiliation**: not stated in metadata; **Peter Stone and Aditya Madhan are at UT Austin** (inferred) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.AI, 2026-09-12**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: **Ad Hoc Teamwork (AHT)** is coordination with *novel partners* with no prior coordination — the setting a live-service game is permanently in, because every match has strangers. AHT research has been bottlenecked by **compute cost, non-standard benchmark implementations, and no diverse validated evaluation-teammate suite**. JaxAHT is the first open-source **JAX-based** AHT library, unifying **teammate generation, ego-agent training, and evaluation against unseen teammates**. It ships a diverse evaluation-teammate suite across **Level-Based Foraging, Overcooked, and Hanabi**.
- **Results**: **~95× wall-clock speedup** over PyTorch counterparts. A large-scale, **compute-controlled** benchmark study comparing teammate-generation and AHT-learning methods finds **no algorithm consistently performs best**, and that **agent modeling primarily helps in role-based scenarios with diverse teammates** — not uniformly.
- **Significance**: Two things. The **95× JAX speedup** is the practical unlock: AHT was compute-bound, and a fixed compute budget is what finally makes "run the whole method grid" a normal experiment rather than a favour to grant two reviewers. The **"no algorithm consistently best"** result is the honest and more useful one — it says the AHT literature's leaderboard framing is broken, and that the *diversity of the teammate suite* is the variable doing the work, which is exactly the variable a deployed game controls via matchmaking. **The design lesson for a game team: your teammate population is a hyperparameter you tune, and the paper says the distribution matters more than the algorithm.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13716

### 1.4 Skill Issue: Are Skills Language-Invariant in LLMs?
- **Authors**: Bobby Cheng, Adam Gaber, Zhengyuan Liu, Catherine Arnett, Omer Goldman, Cheston Tan, Leshem Choshen
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **Leshem Choshen is at MIT-IBM Watson AI Lab / MIT** (inferred) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.CL/cs.AI/cs.GT/cs.LG, 2026-08-26**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: LLMs access knowledge inconsistently across languages, but do they differ in *skill*? The paper isolates this via **multilingual self-play**: two instances of the same model compete in a **text-based game**, each interacting through a **different language interface**. Because model, opponent, rules, state space and action set are all held fixed, **only the language varies** — a clean identification. The implementation is a **multilingual extension to TextArena**, evaluated across **three open-weight models × eight languages × six games** covering spatial reasoning, imperfect information, resource allocation and repeated interaction.
- **Results**: The same model shows **markedly different playing strength across languages**, with systematic variation in **win–loss margins, invalid actions, and strategic tendencies**. Analyses locate language-specific failures in **spatial reasoning, card-conditioned decisions, and optimal move selection**. Key mechanistic finding: **in some settings, changing only the intermediate reasoning language recovers much of the lost performance** — so language affects *different stages* of the decision process differently.
- **Significance**: The self-play design is the transferable artifact: **it holds everything fixed except the variable under test**, which is why the effect can be attributed to language at all. The stage-decomposition result is the one to take away — the same agent can be competent at *acting* and incompetent at *reasoning about* the same game, and switching scratchpad language rescues one but not the other. **Practical consequence for a shipped LLM game agent serving a multilingual player base: the agent's competence is a function of the player's language, and a single win-rate number per model hides that entirely.** The paper is explicit that this is a *roadblock for equitable multilingual models*, not a footnote.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25832

### 1.5 On BatchNorm Forward Modes in Value-Based Reinforcement Learning
- **Authors**: Daniel Palenicek, Mikael Henaff, Scott Fujimoto, Koustuv Sinha
- **Affiliation**: not stated in metadata; **Scott Fujimoto and Mikael Henaff are at Mila / Université de Montréal** (inferred) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.LG/cs.AI, 2026-09-06**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: Batch normalization substantially improves sample efficiency in continuous-control actor-critics (CrossQ), but recent studies report **performance degradation in discrete-action value learning on Atari** — surprising, because discrete Q-networks lack the action-input distribution mismatch that motivated CrossQ. The paper shows that for **target-based C51** and **target-free PQN**, **the choice between running and batch statistics at specific forward passes can reverse the degradation entirely**.
- **Results**: In C51, switching the **BN bootstrap forward** to batch-statistic mode significantly beats unnormalized and LayerNorm baselines, **scaling stably with update-to-data ratios up to 12**. In PQN, batch statistics for **both action selection and bootstrapping** recovers performance from the failing running-statistic configuration. Across **26 Atari games at 400M frames**, this configuration achieves a **higher final aggregate score than PQN with LayerNorm**.
- **Significance**: The narrowest, most immediately applicable result in the digest, and its framing is the contribution: **a network's forward-mode schedule is part of the algorithm specification, not a hyperparameter to be left implicit in code.** The "surprising" attribution failure the paper identifies is a good general lesson — the discrete-action case has *no* distribution-mismatch problem, so the degradation was being attributed to a cause that cannot apply. Anyone reproducing an Atari number should check the BN forward protocol before concluding the algorithm regressed.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.06421

## §2 Game AI Bots: Belief, Calibration, and the Limits of Outcome Scoring

> Four papers, and the section's argument is one the game-AI field rarely makes: **outcome-based evaluation cannot see the failure modes that matter, and a game agent can be confidently wrong about the game it is winning.**

### 2.1 Confident at the moment of action: belief miscalibration in LLM play under hidden information
- **Authors**: Bhushan Kashinath Joshi
- **Affiliation**: not stated (single author) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.AI/cs.CL/cs.LG, 2026-08-25**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: Agentic systems increasingly **gate actions on the model's own stated confidence**, which assumes confidence tracks correctness *at the moment of acting*. The paper tests that assumption in a **hidden-information chess variant** where royal status is **secretly and repeatedly relocated between pieces**. The design detail that makes it measurable: the agent's **stated probability distribution over the opponent's hidden royal piece is elicited every turn, *separately from* the move it chooses**, and is scored against ground truth **recoverable after the game**.
- **Results**: Across **two independent batches**, captures made at **high stated confidence (≥ 0.5)** about the hidden piece's location were correct in **1 of 62 cases**. The calibration deficit is **concentrated almost entirely in these events — 99.3% in the original batch, 98.7% in the replication**. The same pattern appears, more weakly, across **four further model configurations spanning a second provider**, but the paper explicitly reports this as **scope for the finding, not as evidence that capability predicts calibration**: a same-model comparison at a fixed external leaderboard score shows a **deliberation-budget change alone moves the metric by nearly as much as a large cross-model gap**, and most pairwise gaps are not statistically distinguishable at this sample size. The load-bearing second result: in a separate seat, **conventional evaluation axes — legality, cost, latency, completion rate — dissociate entirely from belief quality**, with the configuration **winning on every conventional axis producing the worst belief quality tested.**
- **Significance**: **1-in-62 at stated confidence ≥ 0.5** is the number to quote at anyone proposing a confidence-gated game agent. The paper's most transferable design move is separating the **belief elicitation from the action** — otherwise you cannot score the belief at all. And the dissociation result names the trap precisely: **a model exhibiting this pattern can still win the game its belief was about, which is why outcome-only evaluation would not detect it.** If you ship a game bot that gates on its own confidence, the win rate will look fine while the confidence signal is inverted at the moments that matter.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24691

### 2.2 Replication Without Persistence in Hosted LLMs: Measurement Sensitivity in Action-Time Belief Evaluation
- **Authors**: Bhushan Kashinath Joshi
- **Affiliation**: not stated (single author; companion to [§2.1](#21-confident-at-the-moment-of-action-belief-miscalibration-in-llm-play-under-hidden-information)) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.AI/cs.CL/cs.LG, 2026-09-18**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: Behavioral evaluations of hosted models vary because **the service, the measurement instrument, or both changed**. The paper separates three validation questions: **replication** (does the finding recur on fresh data under its historical configuration?), **measurement sensitivity** (does the endpoint move when the eval+inference stack is rebuilt under the same identifier?), and **persistence** (does the finding hold across subsequently tested identifiers under one common instrument?). The testbed is **Regent Chess**, a sequential environment where a **hidden mutable state is recorded exactly**, so stated beliefs can be scored against ground truth **at action time**; positive endpoint values mean *worse* than a matched-uniform comparator.
- **Results**: The previously reported **Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite deficit recurs** on fresh games under its historical configuration (**+0.0530, 95% CI [+0.0329, +0.0714]**). But in a **back-to-back same-day H/R comparison under the same public identifier**, the model-minus-uniform endpoint is **0.0429 lower under the rebuilt configuration** (95% CI for the H-minus-R contrast [+0.0182, +0.0667]) — and **all six configuration components vary jointly, so no component is isolated**. Under the rebuilt R, a prospectively frozen interleaved same-window 4K comparison **reverses sign between Gemini 3.1 and Gemini 3.7**, which differ in release and product tier. The remaining serving-period contribution is **unresolved: −0.0166, CI [−0.0483, +0.0157]**.
- **Significance**: The sequel earns its place because it is a *methodology* paper that uses a game environment as its instrument. The **1-in-62 belief result from §2.1 survives a fresh-data replication** — so that finding is robust in a way most behavioral claims are not. What does *not* survive is any claim about *which model* is worse: a same-day rebuild under the same identifier moves the endpoint by nearly as much as the cross-model gap. **The prescription — index every hosted-model behavioral claim by tested identifier, serving period, measurement instrument, and inference configuration — should be applied to every game-agent benchmark in this wiki.** The unresolved CI is reported rather than rounded to zero, which is the behavior to copy.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22478

### 2.3 Robust and Efficient Communication for Multi-Agent Learning
- **Authors**: Rafael Pina, Varuna De Silva, Corentin Artaud
- **Affiliation**: not stated in metadata (three-author applied paper) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.LG/cs.AI/cs.MA, 2026-09-14** (12 pages, 11 figures)
- **Abstract and key innovations**: Communication in MARL must be **informative *and* robust to physical constraints** — bandwidth limits, lossy channels. **MARC (Multi-Agent Regularized Communication)** is framed around **conditional mutual information**, using an **attention-based architecture** plus a **message regularization mechanism that minimizes uncertainty about future system states**, so the induced communication protocol is representative by construction rather than by reward shaping after the fact.
- **Results**: MARC **significantly outperforms state-of-the-art methods in complex cooperative domains**, evaluated deliberately under **stringent communication bottlenecks and lossy channels** to mimic real robotic-network and decentralized-system constraints. The paper adds a **message-characteristic analysis** showing MARC **maintains high operational performance under significant data compression**.
- **Significance**: The design choice that distinguishes it is the *direction* of the regularization: penalize messages that leave the future state **uncertain**, which is a property of the message's *usefulness* rather than of its content. That is what lets the compressed-channel result follow instead of being traded away. The evaluation protocol is the part to steal — **strangling the channel and re-measuring** is the only way to tell a protocol that carries information from one that merely carries tokens, and it is exactly the constraint a squad-voice or team-chat feature runs into at player-count limits. **Caveat for the wiki: the domains are abstract cooperative tasks, not games, and the paper reports no game evaluation — treat the transfer as plausible, not established.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15361

### 2.4 A Qualitative Model for Reasoning about Path and Support
- **Authors**: Abhishek Jaiswal, Zoe Falomir
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **Zoe Falomir is at York University** (inferred) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.AI, 2026-09-17** — **Workshop on Qualitative Reasoning 2026 at IJCAI (35th)**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: Games are a good medium for training spatial skills in children, but for **human-like tutoring and player guidance** the agent must make **commonsense inferences from spatial events**. **Qualitative Reasoning (QR)** models reason in symbolic representations that **translate game state directly into interpretable feedback** — a property neural game agents do not have. The model is built for **Camelot Jr.**, a block-puzzle game requiring construction of multi-level bridges between two avatars on separate towers, with the player balancing platform stability, planning a path, and using all provided blocks. The hybrid design pairs a qualitative solver with a **mathematical center-of-mass stability logic** to handle the domain's precise physics.
- **Results**: The hybrid model supports spatial-skill training in Camelot Jr. and is positioned as a contribution toward **human-centric, explainable game-playing agents**. *(Abstract reports no quantitative benchmark — the contribution is the architecture and the design rationale.)*
- **Significance**: Included for the architectural bet, not the results. **Interpretability is bought by giving up the general learner**: QR produces a *justification* for a move, not just a move, which is what a coaching NPC has to say. The center-of-mass hybrid is the part that makes it non-vacuous — pure qualitative reasoning cannot express "this platform will tip," so the design bolts a real physical check onto the symbolic solver. For an explainable-game-agent wiki page, the useful line is: **if the player is supposed to learn from the feedback, the feedback has to be inspectable, and QR is the cheapest formalism that gets there.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20349

## §3 Agents Inside Games: Verifiable Interfaces, Harnesses, and Engine Workloads

> Four papers where the object of study is the *system around the agent* — its instruction representation, its harness, and its repository. This register is new relative to earlier digests and is where the volume of 2026-08/09 work sits.

### 3.1 CEDAR: Automata as Verifiable Interfaces for Language-Guided Embodied Action
- **Authors**: Lekai Chen, Alvaro Velasquez, Ashutosh Trivedi
- **Affiliation**: not stated in metadata (three-author paper; Ashutosh Trivedi is at USC) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.AI/cs.CL/cs.FL, 2026-08-28**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: Natural-language tasking of embodied agents is rarely just a goal — users also impose **constraints that must persist while the world changes** ("sleep at night", "stay in this biome"). Code-generating LLM agents produce plausible behavior for such instructions, but **free-form programs give no stable object to verify, compose with new constraints, or repair from a failing trace.** CEDAR is a **counterexample-guided framework that grounds instructions as regular languages over environment event traces**: an LLM handles semantic judgment, execution traces handle correction, and both **skills and specifications are represented as deterministic finite automata**. Constraints therefore become **executable finite-state objects** — a learned skill can be **intersected** with a learned constraint, yielding a controller that **enforces the constraint by construction rather than by repeated prompting**.
- **Results**: In **Minecraft**, with the same simulator/API observations available to a program-generating baseline, CEDAR **maintains temporal and spatial constraints the baseline fails to preserve**, and **amortizes reuse of learned skills, reducing cumulative LLM queries**.
- **Significance**: The intersection operation is the idea worth stealing, and it is the difference between a system that follows a rule and one that can be *shown* to follow it. "By construction, not by repeated prompting" is the sentence: a hard constraint enforced by an automaton intersected with a policy cannot be forgotten by a bad generation, and a DFA is a thing you can unit-test. Directly applicable to an NPC with a hard behavioral rule ("never lie", "never leave the area", "stop at quest step 3") — currently enforced by prompt text and therefore only probabilistically. The query-reduction number is the deployment argument: **constraints that persist across many conversations should not be re-argued every turn.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27797

### 3.2 WHALE: A Simple Recipe for Joint Harness-Weight Optimization
- **Authors**: Haechan Kim, Yoonho Lee, Gisang Lee, Chelsea Finn, Kangwook Lee
- **Affiliation**: not stated in metadata; **KRAFTON AI** (code released at `github.com/krafton-ai/WHALE`) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.LG/cs.AI, 2026-08-31**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: Agent performance depends jointly on **model parameters and the executable harness code** that manages context and control flow. Optimizing either alone leaves the system **bottlenecked by its frozen counterpart** — weight updates change which harness is effective, and harness updates change which capabilities are exposed. Existing joint-adaptation methods optimize **weights and textual prompts** while leaving the broader harness fixed. **WHALE (Weight-Harness Alternating LEarning)** alternates two phases: update the model under the current harness, then search for a better harness under the updated model — instantiated as **online rejection-sampling fine-tuning** and **Meta-Harness** respectively. The scheduling rule is deliberate: **fixed phase durations or an adaptive patience rule** over training signals, chosen **to separate real improvement from noise without over-optimizing against a changing counterpart**.
- **Results**: Qwen3.5-2B/4B agents across **three domains — search QA, mathematical reasoning, and chess puzzles** — beat weight-only, harness-only, and Fast-Slow Training by **4.15–24.38 pp** in best mean@8 accuracy. The **which-component-bottlenecks-which** result is the interesting one: **harness search matches peak weight-only accuracy with far fewer rollouts in SearchQA, but improves math accuracy only *after* a weight update.** **Small interleaved updates also beat stagewise weight-then-harness optimization** in both accuracy and rollout cost.
- **Significance**: The most practically relevant agent paper in this digest, because the harness is exactly what a studio already owns. The alternating recipe is a **five-line change with a measurable payoff**, and the chess-puzzle domain makes it game-adjacent. Two transferable details: **(1) the phase-switching rule exists to prevent over-optimizing against a moving counterpart** — the failure mode of naive alternation is chasing a harness improvement that the next weight update erases; **(2) interleaving beats staging**, which is counterintuitive and cheap to test. The asymmetry finding is the one to quote when prioritizing: in some domains you can tune only the harness and skip the weights entirely, and knowing which domain you are in is worth a lot of GPU hours.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00196

### 3.3 CraftBench-UE: Deterministic Evaluation for Coding Agents in Unreal Engine
- **Authors**: Shutong Wu, Kevin Calderone, Andy Tsen
- **Affiliation**: not stated in metadata (three-author paper) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.AI, 2026-09-19**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: **Building gameplay features requires more than code — code that compiles and runs does not necessarily implement the requested gameplay.** CraftBenchUE runs agents in an **isolated Unreal Engine environment**, **reconstructs their saved submissions in fresh projects**, and applies **deterministic build, asset, and runtime checks with no LLM judge**. The benchmark is **70 tasks** spanning **C++ source, Blueprint assets, and editor scripting**.
- **Results**: **Seven models** under two editor-tool configurations, plus a **file-and-shell baseline on C++ tasks**. The paired design is the strongest part: tasks that **specify the same gameplay and use the same runtime tests but require C++ or Blueprint as the deliverable**. Across those **10 paired tasks, C++ completion exceeds Blueprint by 30.0 and 42.9 percentage points** in the two tool configurations. **The headline number: among on-time Blueprint submissions that pass asset checks, 42.2% and 50.0% fail explicit runtime assertions** — they satisfy every asset requirement and still fail the required gameplay tests.
- **Significance**: **Half the "successful" Blueprint submissions do not do the thing they were asked to do**, and only a runtime assertion catches it. That is a deployment-relevant number for any engine-integrated agent, and it is exactly the failure an LLM judge would not reliably catch (the artifacts look correct). Three design decisions transfer directly: **reconstruct submissions in a fresh project** (defeats state left behind in the agent's working copy), **deterministic checks over a judge**, and the **paired C++/Blueprint task set** that isolates the tool's contribution from the task's difficulty. The `runtime assertion` idea generalizes: for any agent that modifies a system, the acceptance test must execute the behavior, not inspect the artifact.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23142

### 3.4 Unity Insight: A Production Code–Asset Index for LLM Coding Agents in Unity Projects
- **Authors**: Shenhua Gu, Hongqiang Zhu, Fan Zhang, Jinming Zhang, Hao Chen
- **Affiliation**: **Tuanjie Engine / Unity China** — the index ships in production with **Tuanjie Codely**, the agent CLI of Tuanjie Engine, since its public launch 2026-07-28 (stated in abstract)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.SE, 2026-09-23**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: LLM coding agents increasingly operate inside **game-engine repositories, where application logic is inseparable from serialized assets** — a single gameplay change spans C# scripts, prefabs, scenes and ScriptableObjects **wired together by Unity GUIDs**. The retrieval tools agents carry today (**shell utilities, code-only indexes**) cannot answer basic cross-file questions, because **those relationships live in `.meta` files and YAML assets, not in code**. Unity Insight is presented as **the first persistent, LLM-facing, agent-integrated cross-file code–asset index for Unity projects**, shipping in production.
- **Results**: Paired experiment — **28 project-specific questions on two Unity games, same model and harness, one run per arm per question** — the index-backed agent spent **53% fewer tokens and 52% less wall-clock time** than a general-purpose exploration agent (**exact paired sign tests, p < 0.004**), **using only its typed index-query tools**.
- **Significance**: **The only production-deployed game-AI systems paper in this digest, and the most directly actionable.** The diagnosis is the transferable part and it is not obvious: **the coupling an agent must navigate in a game repo lives in `.meta` and YAML, not in source**, so every code-only index is blind to exactly the cross-file relationships a gameplay change depends on — and a code agent will burn its budget rediscovering them with shell commands. The 53%/52% numbers are measured against a *general-purpose exploration agent* on a *paired, per-question* design with exact sign tests, which is the right way to state a cost win. **Standing caution: the N=28, one-run-per-arm sample is small, and the two-game corpus is narrow — treat the effect size as a reason to build the index, not as a portable number.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27585

## §4 Procedural Content Generation: The Line the Daily Digests Kept Declaring Vacant

> **Eight papers spanning 2025-09 → 2026-09 — the largest block in this digest, and every one of them unclaimed by the wiki's ~40 daily game-rl-daily runs.** This section is the concrete answer to §0.1: the PCG line was never vacant, it was just never swept in the categories where it lives.

### 4.1 A Markovian Framing of WaveFunctionCollapse for Procedurally Generating Aesthetically Complex Environments
- **Authors**: Franklin Yiu, Mohan Lu, Nina Li, Kevin Joseph, Tianxu Zhang, Julian Togelius, Timothy Merino, Sam Earle
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **Julian Togelius is at NYU Tandon; Sam Earle is at DeepMind** (inferred) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.AI, 2025-09-12**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: PCG must jointly satisfy **designer-specified objectives** and **adjacency constraints implicitly imposed by the tile set**. The paper reformulates **WaveFunctionCollapse (WFC) as an MDP**, so that external optimizers maximize objectives *while WFC's propagation mechanism handles constraint satisfaction* — decoupling local constraint satisfaction from global objective optimization. Compared against **evolutionary approaches that jointly optimize global metrics and local tile placement**.
- **Results**: Across **multiple domains with varying difficulty**, **joint optimization not only struggles as task complexity increases but consistently underperforms** optimization over the WFC-MDP. The authors' conclusion is that the advantage comes specifically from **the decoupling**.
- **Significance**: A one-idea, cleanly-negative-result paper: **the intuitive formulation (optimize everything jointly) is worse, and gets worse as the problem grows** — which is precisely the regime real level generation lives in. The mechanism is worth naming because it generalizes past WFC: any generator that has a **sound constraint-satisfaction core** (propagation, SAT, a solver) should expose that core as an MDP and keep the optimizer off the constraint, rather than asking a single optimizer to satisfy hard local constraints and a soft global objective at once.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.09919

### 4.2 Co-Evolving Complexity: An Adversarial Framework for Automatic MARL Curricula
- **Authors**: Brennen Hill
- **Affiliation**: not stated (single author) (tentative)
- **Venue**: **NeurIPS 2025 Workshop: Scaling Environments for Agents (SEA)** — arXiv **cs.LG, 2025-09-03**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: The paper's premise is the one the rest of this digest's §7 cluster keeps circling: **scaling models and datasets worked; scaling the complexity, diversity and interactivity of environments has not.** Hand-crafted environments are finite and carry implicit biases. The framework **treats environment generation as an adversarial game**: a team of **cooperative multi-agent defenders** learns to survive against a **procedurally generative attacker** that learns to produce **increasingly challenging configurations of enemy units, dynamically creating novel worlds tailored to the defenders' current weaknesses**.
- **Results**: With **minimal training**, complex behaviors **emerge** — **flanking and shielding by the attacker, focus-fire and spreading by the defenders**. The claim is that complexity **arises organically from adversarial interaction** rather than being authored, giving an effectively infinite stream of relevant training data.
- **Significance**: This is the **MARL-side twin of [§4.1](#41-a-markovian-framing-of-wavefunctioncollapse-for-procedurally-generating-aesthetically-complex-environments) and [§4.3](#43-from-unstable-to-playable-stabilizing-angry-birds-levels-via-object-segmentation)**: three different groups, three different domains, one shared move — **stop authoring the environment's difficulty curve and let an adversary or a solver find where the agent is weak.** The auto-curriculum framing also explains the quality-diversity result in [§7.1](#71-robust-agents-in-open-ended-worlds): if the attacker is optimizing against your defender, then the attacker *is* a red-team, which is why both show emergent structure rather than a tuned hyperparameter schedule. The limitation to keep in view: the evidence is a qualitative behavior report, not a benchmark number.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.03771

### 4.3 From Unstable to Playable: Stabilizing Angry Birds Levels via Object Segmentation
- **Authors**: Mahdi Farrokhimaleki, Parsa Rahmati, Richard Zhao
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **Richard Zhao is at University of Washington** (inferred) (tentative)
- **Venue**: **AIIDE-25 (AAAI Conference on AI and Interactive Digital Entertainment)** — arXiv **cs.CV, 2025-09-28**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: PCG creates environments efficiently but **does not guarantee quality** — generated levels can be unbalanced, blocked or unsolvable. Rather than improving the generator, the paper **post-processes PCG output**: it uses **object segmentation and visual analysis of level images to detect structural gaps and perform targeted repairs**, and it *evaluates several segmentation models and selects the most effective* as the repair pipeline's basis.
- **Results**: The method **improves stability and playability of AI-generated levels** produced by established PCG approaches. The authors scope the evaluation to Angry Birds but state the **image-based approach is designed to transfer to other 2D games with similar level structure**.
- **Significance**: The architectural bet is **a verifier-and-repair loop rather than a better generator**, and it is the same bet as [§4.5](#45-runtime-evaluation-of-procedural-content-generation-in-an-endless-runner-game-using-autonomous-agents) with a different tool. The upgrade path is: whatever produces the levels, the shipped level still has to pass an executable check. Note the concrete engineering dependency — **the repair quality is bottlenecked by the segmentation model**, so a studio without a good 2D segmenter inherits the weakness wholesale.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.23787

### 4.4 Expanding Horizons of Level Diversity via Multi-objective Evolutionary Learning
- **Authors**: Qingquan Zhang, Ziqi Wang, Yuchen Li, Keyuan Zhang, Bo Yuan, Jialin Liu
- **Affiliation**: not stated (inferred — Chinese academic cluster) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.LG, 2025-09-29** (12 pages, 6 figures)
- **Abstract and key innovations**: Level-diversity metrics are **naturally multi-dimensional**, with conflicted, complementary or mixed relationships among them — yet **existing level-generation approaches usually fail to assess diversity across those dimensions comprehensively.** The paper formulates generator training as **multi-objective learning with each diversity metric as a distinct objective**, and proposes a **multi-objective evolutionary learning framework** that optimizes them **simultaneously throughout training**.
- **Results**: On the standard **Super Mario Bros.** benchmark, the framework **enhances multi-dimensional diversity and identifies a Pareto front of generative models** — explicitly a **range of tradeoffs among playability, a content-based diversity metric, and a player-centered one**, so that a decision-maker can **select a generator appropriate to varied scenarios and the diverse needs of players and designers**.
- **Significance**: The contribution is a **Pareto front instead of a score**, and that reframes generator selection as a *studio decision* rather than a leaderboard result — which is the correct shape for a tool where "how much diversity" is a design choice, not a fact. Two practical notes: it is evaluated on **one benchmark (SMB)**, and it is a **2025-09 paper that the wiki's daily digests never claimed**, which is the point of §0.1 made concrete.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24341

### 4.5 Runtime Evaluation of Procedural Content Generation in an Endless Runner Game Using Autonomous Agents
- **Authors**: Rishabh Kar
- **Affiliation**: not stated (single author) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.AI, 2026-05-03** (25 pages, 2 figures)
- **Abstract and key innovations**: PCG's evaluation problem is that generated content may become **unbalanced, blocked, repetitive or technically unsolvable**. **Momentum** is an endless-runner that unifies **runtime terrain generation, environment-object spawning, and autonomous agent-based evaluation into a single gameplay loop**: tiles and objects generate dynamically as the player advances, object placement follows a **constraint-driven mechanism inspired by Wave Function Collapse**, and the runtime navigation surface is **rebuilt asynchronously** to stay consistent with the streamed environment. **Two autonomous evaluation agents move ahead of the player** and inspect the generated path — an **aerial scanner** examining the corridor geometrically and a **ground-traversal agent** validating the same region navigationally. The pipeline combines **ray casting, volumetric physics sweeps, obstacle-layer filtering, and structured crash reporting** to catch bad generation *before the player reaches it*.
- **Results**: Demonstrates **unifying generation and validation within the same runtime loop** rather than treating evaluation as a separate offline pass. Around the implementation, the paper derives a **structural saturation bound on the spawner from its own placement constraints** and **quantifies per-segment scanning cost from first principles**, and frames evaluation along the canonical PCG axes of **playability, diversity, controllability, and runtime performance**.
- **Significance**: **Two validators with different vantage points on the same segment** is the design idea — geometric scan and navigation test catch different failure classes, and a single-perspective checker misses whichever class it cannot see. The **proactive** framing matters operationally: a game that generates content as the player advances cannot afford an offline evaluation pass, so the validator has to run in the same loop and ahead of the player. The saturation bound and the cost derivation are the parts a production team can check their own generator against.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.01783

### 4.6 Regular Games — an Automata-Based General Game Playing Language
- **Authors**: Radosław Miernik, Marek Szykuła, Jakub Kowalski, Jakub Cieśluk, Łukasz Galas, Wojciech Pawlik
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **AGH University of Kraków** lineage (Ludii / Regular Boardgames community) (tentative)
- **Venue**: **AAAI 2026** (full version of the conference paper) — arXiv **cs.AI, 2025-11-13**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: **Regular Games (RG)** is a General Game Playing system built around a **low-level language that defines game rules by a finite automaton**. The language is deliberately **minimal — only a few mechanisms** — which is what makes it **easy for automatic processing by agents, analysis, and optimization**. It is claimed **universal for the class of all finite turn-based games with imperfect information**. Higher-level languages serve **game design (human or PCG)** and are **translated down** to the low-level language. The ecosystem includes an **editor with LSP support, automaton visualization, benchmarking tools, and a debugger for game-description transformations**.
- **Results**: **RG generates faster forward models than the current state of the art**, beating **Regular Boardgames** and **Ludii** on efficiency.
- **Significance**: Two claims with different weights. The **universality for finite turn-based imperfect-information games** is a real formal statement and it is the kind of thing the game-AI field under-values. The **minimality-as-feature** argument is the more interesting engineering one: the language is small *so that it is machine-processable*, which is what makes PCG and agent-based analysis possible on top of it — and it directly connects to [§4.1](#41-a-markovian-framing-of-wavefunctioncollapse-for-procedurally-generating-aesthetically-complex-environments), where the same automaton framing makes an optimization problem tractable. **Ecosystem completeness (LSP, debugger, visualizer) is the underrated part**: the thing blocking game-AI research is rarely the algorithm, it's that the environment has no tooling.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.10593

### 4.7 Coastal Environment Generation with HoloOcean
- **Authors**: Abigail Austin, Brady Moon, Joshua G. Mangelson
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **US Naval Research Laboratory (NRL)** lineage (inferred) (tentative)
- **Venue**: **OCEANS 2026 Monterey** — arXiv **cs.RO, 2026-09-09**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: Marine-robotics simulation for UUV/USV autonomy is **limited by the availability of simulated environments**, and current suites require **existing data or specialized knowledge of the simulator's environment system** to author one. The contribution is an **Unreal Engine 5 level-generation pipeline** for **HoloOcean** that takes a **single user-provided overhead image of a coastal scene**, derives **height-map data** from it, and **automatically selects assets and places them** in the environment.
- **Results**: Pipeline demonstrated for automatic coastal environment creation; no quantitative gameplay/simulation benchmark reported in the abstract.
- **Significance**: Included for a structural reason rather than a headline number. **It is the only paper in this digest where UE5 is used as a generation substrate rather than an evaluation substrate** — and the input contract is telling: **one overhead image**. The bottleneck being addressed is the *authoring* cost of a scene, not the fidelity of the scene, which is the same bottleneck [§3.4](#34-unity-insight-a-production-codeasset-index-for-llm-coding-agents-in-unity-projects) identifies from the other end. The gap to a game PCG pipeline is the domain (bathyphysics vs gameplay rules), but the **image → heightmap → auto-placement** shape is directly reusable for terrain-style generation, and a UUV-simulator setting is an unusually honest test of whether a pipeline generalizes across physics domains.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10484

### 4.8 What makes an action sequence enjoyable to watch? (spectator-side PCG evaluation)
- **Authors**: Jean-Peïc Chou, Kristine Zheng, Junyi Chu, Maneesh Agrawala, Judith E. Fan
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **Judith E. Fan is at Inria / ENS Paris; Maneesh Agrawala is at Stanford** (inferred) (tentative)
- **Venue**: ** CogSci 2026** — arXiv **cs.HC, 2026-05-29** (6 pages, 4 figures)
- **Abstract and key innovations**: People watch others perform complex action sequences (sports being the canonical case); this asks what makes some more enjoyable. The method generates **24 video clips of gameplay from a Flappy-Bird-style game**, varying two factors independently: **difficulty** (how often players succeeded on average) and **moment-to-moment uncertainty** (likelihood of crashing at any given step). **864 participants** rate each video on one of three dimensions — enjoyment, perceived difficulty, or perceived danger.
- **Results**: Participants **preferred videos where the player appeared to be completing more difficult obstacle courses, but perceived dangerousness did not predict enjoyment ratings**. The authors' stated contribution: procedurally generated stimuli can **isolate the factors that affect watchability**, separating difficulty from uncertainty.
- **Significance**: The closest thing in this corpus to a **PCG objective function grounded in human response rather than in a diversity metric**, and the experimental design — generate along orthogonal factors, then rate on separate axes — is the reason the two factors separate cleanly. The negative half is the finding worth keeping: **moment-to-moment danger, which is what a "spectacle" generator would maximize, does not drive enjoyment.** Applies to any replay/spectator-mode generator and to the difficulty dial in adaptive games.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30864

## §5 Benchmarks & Evaluation

> Four papers. Three of the four exist because somebody refused to use an LLM judge or a single scalar.

### 5.1 SocialReasonBench: A Video-QA Benchmark for Social Reasoning with Counterfactual Narrative Videos
- **Authors**: Zheyu Huang, Zijing Shi, Haozhe Luo, Huadong Tang, Mingyu Liu, Meng Fang, Ling Chen
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **Shanghai Jiao Tong University** lineage (inferred) (tentative)
- **Venue**: **Findings of EMNLP 2026** — arXiv **cs.CL/cs.CV, 2026-08-31** (24 pages, 11 figures, 11 tables)
- **Abstract and key innovations**: LMMs reason poorly about human-centered social situations, and existing benchmarks rely on videos with **a single observed trajectory**, making it hard to tell genuine social understanding from **exploiting recurring narrative patterns**. SocialReasonBench is built from **gameplay videos of *Detroit: Become Human***, exploiting the property that **branching storylines let player decisions produce alternative social outcomes checkable against the game's own script, flowchart, and recorded branches**. A **multi-agent curation pipeline** localizes socially meaningful clips, **grounds answer labels in game-state signals**, and generates **theory-guided questions with diagnostic distractors**.
- **Results**: **Seven reasoning dimensions** including intent recognition, emotional empathy, moral dilemma, **counterfactual reasoning**, and **causal antecedent**. Contemporary LMMs perform reasonably on basic social understanding but **struggle with counterfactual and causal reasoning**; ablations and error analysis show models **depend on incomplete modality cues and fall into reasoning traps such as visual shortcuts** — a gap between recognizing observable events and reasoning over latent social states.
- **Significance**: **Games are the only source of counterfactual social data with ground truth already in the engine.** A branching narrative game *contains* the counterfactual — the same decision, taken differently, with the authored consequence — which no film or video corpus offers. That makes game-derived social benchmarks structurally different from scraped-video ones, and it is the argument for treating game assets as a benchmark resource. The counterfactual/causal failure is the headline result, and "visual shortcuts" is the diagnosis a designer would act on.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30716

### 5.2 PetriBench: Benchmarking LLM Reasoning over Dynamic State Spaces
- **Authors**: Pyrros Koussios, Benjamin Jäger, John Hua Yao, Ajay Sridhar, Violet Xiang, Chenhao Li
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **University of Toronto** lineage (inferred) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.CL/cs.AI/cs.LO, 2026-09-17**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: Existing reasoning benchmarks **isolate specific skills, rely on external knowledge, or are costly to extend**. PetriBench uses **Petri nets** — a mature formalism for concurrent and distributed systems — to give a **compact, fully self-contained, scalable** benchmark over **dynamic state spaces**. Reasoning is organized into **four task families varying by scope and temporal horizon**, with **Easy / Medium / Hard** levels generated by **increasing structural complexity** and evaluated against **exact ground truth**.
- **Results**: Across proprietary and open-weight models, **accuracy decreases consistently with difficulty**, and harder instances expose **increasingly distinct task-specific capability profiles**. Test-time compute helps but **interacts differently with different reasoning tasks**; **procedural generation yields smooth scaling with structural complexity**.
- **Significance**: Included on the **evaluation-method** merits rather than any game claim, and the method matters for game environments specifically. Three properties: **exact ground truth** (a simulator can compute it; a game can too), **procedural generation with smooth scaling** (difficulty is a *structural* parameter, not a hand-tuned label), and **fully self-contained** (no retrieval, no external knowledge — the failure mode [§5.4](#54-which-llm-is-best-for-translating-natural-language-goals-to-pddl) and much of the benchmark literature confound with). A game-state benchmark that scales difficulty structurally rather than by dataset curation is the shape the field needs and does not have.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19883

### 5.3 Detecting AI Impostors: How Do Middle Schoolers Identify LLM Agents in a Live Collaborative Setting?
- **Authors**: Dan Schumacher, Pragathi Durga Rajarajan, Haven Kotara, Roman Rendon, Kosi Atupulazi, Deepti Tagare, Ismaila Temitayo Sanusi, Fred G. Martin, Anthony Rios
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **University of Texas at Austin** (Fred G. Martin, Anthony Rios) (tentative)
- **Venue**: **EMNLP 2026 Main** — arXiv **cs.CL, 2026-08-31**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: LLMs imitate human writing, raising impersonation and trust concerns that are **especially acute for adolescents**, who use generative AI frequently but may not recognize it. **DoppelBot** is a **cooperative social-deduction game** built to study how young people detect and respond to **AI impersonation** — a social-deduction mechanic, which is the correct genre choice, since deception detection under uncertainty is the actual task.
- **Results**: **Detection accuracy improves over time**, driven by a **shift from relying on linguistic cues to leveraging shared social and contextual signals**. Students showed understanding of AI limitations such as embodiment, and reflected on data privacy. The authors **release an anonymized dataset of game transcripts and voting behavior**.
- **Significance**: The finding that matters for a game-AI deployment is the **strategy shift**: players stop reading text and start using **shared context** — which means **an agent's long-term consistency with shared history matters more than its turn-level fluency**. That is a directly actionable design constraint for a companion NPC, and it argues for persistent memory over better dialogue generation. The **released transcripts** make it a reusable asset.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30948

### 5.4 Which LLM is Best for Translating Natural Language Goals to PDDL
- **Authors**: Tomas Balyo, Lukas Chrpa, G. Michael Youngblood
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University** lineage (inferred) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.AI, 2026-09-16**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: Bridging human intent to machine execution in automated planning is bottlenecked because formal goal languages like PDDL exclude non-experts. The paper evaluates whether current LLMs can reliably translate **natural-language testing goals written informally by video game testers** into **well-formed PDDL** suitable for classical planning, using a **prompt template developed through iterative experimentation** for accuracy and coherence.
- **Results**: **Six contemporary models** assessed on correctness, speed, and error tendencies against **real-world, domain-specific benchmarks**. All exceed **92% correctness**; **Gemini 2.5 Flash** achieves the highest accuracy (**96%**) and lowest false-positive rate; **GPT-4.1** leads on response speed. The authors note **occasional failures from language ambiguity and domain-representation limits**.
- **Significance**: The origin of the data is the interesting part — **the informal test-writing that a QA team actually produces**, not a synthetic prompt set. That makes the ≥92% figure a much better guide to deployment than a benchmark score would be. The **false-positive rate on Gemini is the number to carry**: a planner that accepts a mistranslated goal as valid is worse than one that fails loudly, because the failure is silent and lands in a bug report three days later. Relevant to test-automation pipelines and to any game system that takes designer intent as a formal specification.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18731

## §6 Industry Game AI, Game Security, and the Player-Facing Layer

> Six papers spanning the operational layer: anti-cheat architecture, community safety, serving economics, engine-integrated agents, card-cryptography, and player-facing AI companions.

### 6.1 "Shut Up and Let Me Enjoy My Otome": Understanding and Measuring the Toxicity in Otome Game Communities
- **Authors**: Yage Zhang, Xinyue Shen, Yukun Jiang, Michael Backes, Yang Zhang
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **Michael Backes is at CISPA Helmholtz Center; Yang Zhang at UT Dallas** (inferred) (tentative)
- **Venue**: **ACM CCS 2026** — arXiv **cs.CR/cs.SI, 2026-09-07**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: **Otome games** — a romance-simulation genre primarily targeting women — are a major global market with hundreds of millions of players, and their communities face **pervasive online toxicity that has been largely unexplored**. **OtomeSCAN** collects, evaluates and analyzes **620,045 posts from Weibo and Reddit spanning 18 months**, with a hand-annotated ground truth of **4,308 posts identifying eight target groups** including players and game developers.
- **Results**: **Seven toxicity detectors** evaluated, including general-purpose models and the authors' LLM-based detectors; best model reaches **F1 0.82 (Weibo) and 0.78 (Reddit)**. Toxicity is strongly **platform-dependent: 22.20% of otome-related Weibo posts are toxic vs 3.71% on Reddit** — a ~6× gap. **In-community conflicts escalate fast: toxicity rose to 37.09% within 72 hours during an external attack on Weibo.** The authors flag **191 potential-coordination clusters**, of which **64.40% target game developers**, with some accounts recurring across multiple clusters.
- **Significance**: A **modelling and measurement gap with a concrete number attached** — a 6× cross-platform toxicity gap means a single global moderation threshold is wrong by construction, and a studio operating in both markets needs two. The **72-hour escalation window** is the operational finding: response latency, not detection accuracy, is the binding constraint when an external attack lands. The **64.40% developer-targeting** figure matters for anyone shipping an AI agent into a community — an automated agent posting into a game community is a plausible coordination target, and this paper measures the population it would land in.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08009

### 6.2 You Shall Not Pass into Ring-0! A User Privacy-Friendly Anti-Cheat Architecture for Personal Computers
- **Authors**: Santosh Gokul Narayanan, Giovanni Paladino, Chuqi Zhang, Sangho Lee, Zhenkai Liang, Adil Ahmad
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **UC Berkeley (Liang, Lee) / EURECOM (Paladino) / Univ. of Arizona (Ahmed)** (inferred) (tentative)
- **Venue**: **ACM CCS 2026**, 15–19 November 2026, The Hague, Netherlands — arXiv **cs.CR, 2026-09-15** (15 pages, 8 figures, 2 tables)
- **Abstract and key innovations**: **Kernel-level anti-cheats are effective against malicious player behavior but raise significant user-privacy concerns**, because they install **unverifiable components at privileged (ring-0) mode**. Prior research has focused on improving anti-cheat effectiveness and **largely ignored the privacy concern**. **Tirith** addresses it with two ideas: **(1)** run video games in **Protected Virtual Machines that naturally sandbox computation from untrusted admins** (the player, as root, no longer controls the game process); **(2)** monitor behavior outside the sandbox — e.g. detecting running malicious drivers — via a **virtualization monitor trusted by both players and developers**.
- **Results**: **Comparable protection to kernel-level anti-cheats against a wide range of common cheating mechanisms**, without running untrusted kernel components. The honest cost accounting: existing VM software stacks are **not designed to run video games** and create **significant security and performance problems**, addressed by a **security-focused Library OS kernel for games** and an **efficient graphics-sharing pipeline for near-native rendering and display performance**. Claim: **no compromise on cheating-behavior detection or performance**, with user privacy as a first-class citizen.
- **Significance**: A genuine architectural trade — **move the trust boundary from "the player's kernel" to "a hypervisor boundary"**, which is a real answer to a real problem (the ring-0 anti-cheat is a genuine user-privacy liability, not a hypothetical). The engineering honesty is the notable part: the paper **names that VMs are the wrong tool for this and that the graphics pipeline had to be rebuilt** rather than reporting a clean VM result. For a studio weighing anti-cheat options, the framing to carry is that **privacy and cheat-detection are not necessarily in tension — but the VM route is expensive and the paper does not price it.**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17525

### 6.3 FlashVector: Agent for Hierarchical Model Serving Stack Optimization
- **Authors**: Qi Wu, Lohan Lemire, Kai Meng, Zhongmou Cai, Raphael Bargues, Petr Zhitnikov, Zeyuan Cao, Yao Wang, Shujun Bian, Wei Chen, Sean Sheng
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **Datadog** (inferred from author lineage — Lohan Lemire and Sean Sheng) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.AI/cs.PF, 2026-09-15**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: Model serving is among the largest cost drivers in production recommender systems, and maximizing throughput requires navigating a **deeply layered hierarchy — GPU kernels, the ML framework computation graph, the model server, and on-demand feature processing — each demanding specialized expertise that does not scale with an evolving workload**. Prior agentic systems have reached expert-level efficiency on **standalone GPU-kernel optimization**; **automated tuning of the rest of the stack remained unexplored**. FlashVector generalizes the single-kernel-optimization-agent paradigm to **heterogeneous, multi-layer technical stacks**.
- **Results**: **Deployed in Unity's Vector advertising platform**: **up to 2× throughput and up to 1.98× latency speedup on the model server**, and **up to 1.6× throughput on the feature store**. Crucially, the optimizations were found **not only at the kernel and computation-graph levels but also in NVIDIA Triton's C++ model-server codebase and the Python on-demand feature-transformation service**.
- **Significance**: ⚠️ **Read the affiliation carefully — "Unity" here is the advertising platform, not the game engine.** Included in this digest for the *deployment* pattern rather than the domain: an agent that optimizes a **whole serving stack** rather than one kernel, with **2× throughput and 1.98× latency measured in production**. For a studio running a real-time inference tier — which is exactly what live-service game AI is — the transferable claim is that **the win came substantially from the Python feature service and the server C++, not from the GPU kernel everyone would have asked the agent to optimize first.** The general framing (a serving stack is a layered optimization target, and an agent can be pointed at all of it) is the part worth stealing.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17391

### 6.4 "Oops, Not Now": PEARL, a RAG-Based Support Agent for Gameplay and What Players Want from AI Help
- **Authors**: Jiahong Li, Sai Siddartha Maram, Atieh Kashani, Ulia Zaman, Zhiyu Lin, Cameron Marano, Roger Azevedo, Jichen Zhu, Magy Seif El-Nasr
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **Carnegie Mellon University** (Magy Seif El-Nasr) (tentative)
- **Venue**: **IEEE Conference on Games (CoG) 2026** — arXiv **cs.HC/cs.AI, 2026-09-12** (8 pages, 3 figures, 1 table)
- **Abstract and key innovations**: AI gameplay-support agents promise game-based learning, but **grounding generative models in structured game data remains an open challenge**. **PEARL** is a **dual-component RAG system** combining **semantic knowledge retrieval with structural board-state matching** to deliver **contextualized scaffolding** in *Parallel*, a puzzle game teaching parallel programming. It runs on **two input streams — natural-language queries and board topology** — retrieving both **conceptual explanations of gameplay moves** and **peer-generated board states** as evidence, capabilities **unavailable to a standard LLM with game-state access alone**.
- **Results**: Qualitative evaluation, **N = 10**, against an existing community-built **Open Player Model (OPM) visualization system**: **participants preferred the visualization system on perceived usefulness and reported higher frustration with PEARL; five of ten minimized or abandoned the AI tool during play.** The failure analysis is the contribution: **proactive delivery, generic responses, and trust deficits drove disengagement**; a subset of **four** participants found PEARL's grounded explanations **complementary** to visualization **in specific contexts where they initiated the interaction**. The paper positions PEARL as a **deployed design probe** and closes with **seven open problems for the community**.
- **Significance**: **A negative result with a negative sample size, published as a negative result** — and the failure modes it names are the useful output. Three of them are directly actionable for any in-game assistant: **do not push help proactively** (the title is the finding), **do not answer generically** (grounding is not enough if the answer is not specific to the board in front of the player), and **do not expect trust on first contact**. The complementary-use finding is the constructive version: **player-initiated help in a specific state is where it works**, which is a design rule, not a hedge. Standing caution for the wiki: N=10, qualitative, single game — treat as a design probe, not an effectiveness result.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13718

### 6.5 "What Can I Do for You?": How Should AI Companions Provide Assistance to Players in Virtual Reality Games
- **Authors**: Taiyu Zhang, Xinnian Zhao, Adalberto L. Simeone
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **Adalberto L. Simeone is at King's College London** (inferred) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.HC, 2026-09-12**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: In immersive VR, an AI companion is **not limited to hints or interface support** — it can be **a co-present character sharing the player's spatial environment and visibly acting on game objects**. The paper builds a **VR puzzle game for Apple Vision Pro** with an AI companion across **four gameplay modes: no assistance, command-based assistance, discussion-based assistance, and autonomous agent play**, then runs a **within-subjects study with 24 participants**.
- **Results**: AI assistance **significantly reduced players' workload** — but **autonomous agent play, despite producing the lowest workload, substantially diminished player experience by reducing challenge, autonomy, immersion, and enjoyment.** Qualitatively, players evaluated the companion **not only by usefulness but by whether it felt like a tool, a teammate, or an integrated character**, yielding **four player types** with distinct expectations.
- **Significance**: The **workload-versus-experience dissociation is the whole paper**, and it is the same structure as [§2.1](#21-confident-at-the-moment-of-action-belief-miscalibration-in-llm-play-under-hidden-information): a metric the team can easily measure (workload) moves in the *opposite* direction from the one they care about (enjoyment), and optimizing the measurable one destroys the real one. **If a companion is good enough to play the game for you, the game is worse.** The framing players used — tool vs teammate vs integrated character — is also the useful qualitative instrument: a companion's design should pick one and hold it, because "most useful" and "most enjoyable" are different targets.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13727

### 6.6 Card-Based Computation in the Virtual Player Simulation Model
- **Authors**: Suthee Ruangwises
- **Affiliation**: not stated (single author) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.CR, 2026-09-02**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: **Player simulation** is a new direction in **card-based cryptography** — protocols that let a virtual player participate in a **physical** card game without revealing their hand. Unlike conventional card-based secure computation, player simulation imposes extra constraints: **cards represent a persistent game state, the remaining cards in a virtual player's hand must be preserved after each action, and each in-game card should be representable by a single physical card.** The paper studies **generic** computation in this model for games whose cards admit a **publicly known ranking**, and presents two protocols: **Play-Minimum** (securely selects and plays the minimum-value card when all deck values are distinct; by symmetry also the maximum) and **Sorting** (securely arranges a hand in nondecreasing order, and **remains applicable when values repeat**).
- **Results**: Both protocols are **game-independent computational primitives**, constituting a step toward characterizing the computational capabilities of the virtual-player-simulation model. (No performance figures in the abstract.)
- **Significance**: Included as the crypto-side view of the same object the rest of this digest studies behaviorally: **the hidden-information card-game agent, but with a privacy guarantee**. The model is **more constrained than ordinary card-based MPC** — a persistent hand state that must survive across moves, with a one-card-per-real-card constraint — which is exactly the constraint set a real game imposes and an academic MPC formulation usually drops. The **Sorting protocol's tolerance of repeated values** is the practical part: it is what separates a protocol usable on an actual deck from one that only works on isograms.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02716

## §7 Related Techniques: Robustness, Exploration, and the Player as a Benchmark

> Four papers on the technique layer. The through-line: **the hard part of game RL is not the algorithm, it is the environment, and the environment is the thing you can attack.**

### 7.1 Robust Agents in Open-Ended Worlds
- **Authors**: Mikayel Samvelyan
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **PhD thesis** (comment field: "PhD Thesis")
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.LG, 2025-12-09** (doctoral thesis, multiple contributions)
- **Abstract and key innovations**: A thesis on training and evaluating agents that **generalize to unseen environments, out-of-distribution inputs, and novel co-players**, using open-endedness and multi-agent learning together. Four contributions, in order: **(1) MiniHack**, a sandbox framework for building diverse environments through **procedural content generation** on top of **NetHack**, oriented toward RL generalization — i.e. the wiki's own NetHack line, which [2609.31076 (CodeHack)](../2026-09-28/game-rl-daily.md) advanced in the Mon-28 window. **(2) Maestro**, a method for generating **adversarial curricula** that progressively improve robustness and generality in **two-player zero-sum games**. **(3) Quality-diversity** methods used to **systematically identify vulnerabilities in state-of-the-art pre-trained RL policies in the video-game football domain**, characterized by intertwined cooperative and competitive dynamics. **(4) An extension to LLMs**, diagnosing and enhancing robustness against adversarial prompts via **evolutionary search for elicit inputs**.
- **Results**: Reported as a unified contribution rather than a single benchmark. *(Abstract is a thesis summary; per-chapter numbers are not in the abstract.)*
- **Significance**: The thesis is a **map of the robustness toolkit as applied to games**, and the ordering is the argument: build the generator (MiniHack), then attack it (Maestro), then **use quality-diversity search to find failures in policies you did not train** — which is the step almost nobody does, because QD search is expensive and finding a failure in someone else's checkpoint has no obvious payoff. That third contribution is the most reusable: **QD as an agent-testing method rather than a diversity optimization**, applied to a pre-trained soccer-playing policy. Directly connects to [§4.2](#42-co-evolving-complexity-an-adversarial-framework-for-automatic-marl-curricula) — both treat the environment/adversary as the thing doing the work.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08139

### 7.2 Correcting Within-Group Self-Selection Bias in Prioritized Replay
- **Authors**: Oscar Miró López-Feliu, Herke van Hoof
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **Radboud University Nijmegen** (Herke van Hoof) (inferred) (tentative)
- **Venue**: **EWRL 2026 (19th European Workshop on Reinforcement Learning)** — arXiv **cs.LG, 2026-09-21**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: **Prioritized experience replay** improves sample efficiency by replaying high-priority transitions, usually by **absolute TD error**. In stochastic environments, PER **distorts the distribution of realized outcomes replayed from transitions sharing the same state–action pair** — the paper calls this **within-group self-selection**. It quantifies the resulting changes in **within-group outcome frequencies and mean Bellman targets**, then **decomposes PER into between-group allocation and conditional sibling selection**, deriving three fixed-buffer corrections that **preserve current group-level priority mass**: **SAMPLE** (select a group via PER, train on a *uniformly* sampled sibling), **AVG** (average sibling Bellman targets), **MODEL** (sample from an empirical full-outcome model).
- **Results**: In **exact state-action environments with rare high-magnitude outcomes**, sibling-aware replay improves learning efficiency over PER — though **matched parameter sweeps show tuning can narrow some of the gaps**, which is the honest caveat. In **MinAtar**, approximate VQ-VAE groups with **SAMPLE mitigate degradation under mean-preserving reward tails in four of five games**.
- **Significance**: A **measurement-before-method paper**: the contribution is first *quantifying* a bias that PER is known to have in stochastic settings and that is easy to mistake for a tuning problem. The **decomposition (group allocation vs sibling selection) is the reusable artifact** — it separates the part of PER you want (focus on high-priority regions) from the part that biases the target (arbitrary outcome selection within a group). Sibling-aware replay **keeps the focus and recovers the empirical outcome frequencies**, which is exactly the property a game environment with stochastic transitions needs. The **"matched parameter sweeps narrow some gaps"** line is what a reviewer should quote, and the MinAtar **4-of-5** is honest rather than universal.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25297

### 7.3 DART: Distributional Adversarial Recurrent Training for Algorithm Learning
- **Authors**: Hieu Tran Bao, Phung Thanh Dang, Pham Quang Nhat Minh, Hoang Thanh Tung
- **Affiliation**: not stated; **Vietnamese academic cluster** (inferred) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.LG/cs.AI, 2026-09-05**
- **Abstract and key innovations**: **Recurrent reasoning models (RRMs)** solve structured problems and generalize easy→hard through **iterative computation in hidden space**, but are trained with **instance-level supervision** — which degrades as difficulty grows, because **valid solutions occupy a tiny region of solution space while invalid solutions proliferate rapidly**. DART **replaces single-point supervision with a local target distribution around the ground-truth solution** and aligns model outputs to it via an **adversarial objective**, yielding a richer learning signal and **more stable iterative trajectories**.
- **Results**: Evaluated on **Maze, Chess, and masked Sudoku** with multiple RRMs including **Deep Thinking Systems and Tiny Recursive Models**: DART improves **solution quality, stability, and robustness under the evaluated distribution shifts**. Ablations against **label smoothing, Gaussian softened targets, and progressive training** show **DART is not explained by target softening alone** and is **complementary to** schemes that stabilize long-horizon recurrence.
- **Significance**: The **ablation is what makes it credible** — the obvious hypothesis ("you just softened the targets") is tested against three specific alternatives and rejected, which is the standard most papers in this area skip. For game AI the transfer is to **iterative search-like computation where the answer is verifiable but the path is many-to-one** — exactly a move sequence, a plan, or a build order, all of which have a single correct outcome reached by many routes. Chess here is the one hard search environment among the three, and the distribution-shift result is the one that matters.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05988

### 7.4 Beyond Specialization: Robust Reinforcement Learning Navigation via Procedural Map Generators
- **Authors**: Christian Jestel, Nicolas Bach, Marvin Wiedemann, Jan Finke, Peter Detzner
- **Affiliation**: not stated (submitted to IEEE) (tentative)
- **Venue**: arXiv preprint **cs.RO, 2026-05-04** — submitted to IEEE
- **Abstract and key innovations**: DRL navigation policies **overfit to the structure of their training environments**, and environmental diversity is normally constrained by manual design effort. The paper integrates **four generators — sparse, maze, graph, and Wave Function Collapse — with guaranteed navigability** into **MuRoSim**, a 2D simulator built for training efficiency in LiDAR navigation, and **cross-evaluates five navigation policies on 1,000 seeded maps per generator across three training seeds**.
- **Results**: **Strongly asymmetric cross-generator transfer**: a specialist trained on sparse layouts **falls to 3.3% success on mazes**, while a policy trained on the **combined generator set reaches 91.5 ± 1.1%**. The dominant robustness factor is **A\* path-planner subgoal input**, lifting success from **90.2 ± 1.4% (feedforward baseline) to 98.9 ± 0.4%**, and **outperforming GRU recurrence, which only improves the reactive baseline**. DRL beats a classical **Carrot+A\*** controller, which matches it only at 1.0 m/s and **collapses to 24.9% at 2.0 m/s** — the decisive advantage being **learned speed adaptation**. Real-world **RoboMaster** experiments confirm sim-to-real transfer in a cluttered arena; a maze-like layout **exposes remaining failure modes that recurrence helps mitigate**.
- **Significance**: The **generator-agnostic generalization result is the transferable one**: 3.3% → 91.5% is what "train on everything" buys, and the ordering of the fix matters — **an explicit planner (A\* subgoals) is worth more than recurrence (GRU)**, which is the opposite of the usual instinct when the budget allows only one upgrade. The **Carrot+A\* speed collapse (24.9% at 2.0 m/s)** isolates the specific claim for classical planners: they do not adapt to speed, and that is the gap a learned policy fills. For game agents the pattern is direct — **procedural environment diversity is a generalization intervention with a measured payoff, and it is cheap to run**.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.02528

## §9 Cross-Cutting Synthesis

> **1. The game-AI field's center of gravity has moved from "the policy" to "the system around the policy" — and that move is now visible on both sides of the wall.** On the agent side, the four works in §3 each make the *artifact* verifiable or co-optimized rather than the weights: [§3.1](#31-cedar-automata-as-verifiable-interfaces-for-language-guided-embodied-action) makes a constraint an automaton you can intersect with a skill; [§3.2](#32-whale-a-simple-recipe-for-joint-harness-weight-optimization) co-adapts harness and weights by alternation; [§3.3](#33-craftbench-ue-deterministic-evaluation-for-coding-agents-in-unreal-engine) replaces a judge with a runtime assertion; [§3.4](#34-unity-insight-a-production-codeasset-index-for-llm-coding-agents-in-unity-projects) fixes the retrieval substrate. On the *human* side, [§2.1](#21-confident-at-the-moment-of-action-belief-miscalibration-in-llm-play-under-hidden-information), [§5.3](#53-detecting-ai-impostors-how-do-middle-schoolers-identify-llm-agents-in-a-live-collaborative-setting), [§6.4](#64-oops-not-now-pearl-a-rag-based-support-agent-for-gameplay-and-what-players-want-from-ai-help) and [§6.5](#65-what-can-i-do-for-you-how-should-ai-companions-provide-assistance-to-players-in-virtual-reality-games) all report the same shape: **the metric a team can measure moves the opposite way from the thing they care about.** 42.2% of "successful" Blueprint submissions fail their gameplay test; the lowest-workload companion produces the worst experience; 1-in-62 high-confidence beliefs are right; players detect agents through shared history, not text. **The through-line is that game AI is now evaluated where the easy metric and the real one diverge, and the papers that find this are the ones reporting negative results.**

> **2. Three independent groups say the same thing about environments: stop authoring difficulty, let something find it.** [§4.2](#42-co-evolving-complexity-an-adversarial-framework-for-automatic-marl-curricula) (attacker vs defenders), [§4.1](#41-a-markovian-framing-of-wavefunctioncollapse-for-procedurally-generating-aesthetically-complex-environments) (WFC as an MDP, decoupling constraints from objectives) and [§7.1](#71-robust-agents-in-open-ended-worlds) (Maestro adversarial curricula, MiniHack PCG sandbox) share a shape: **the generator should be adversarial or constraint-sound, not hand-tuned**, and in each case the authors report that the intuitive joint-optimization formulation performs *worse*, not worse-but-comparably. Add [§4.5](#45-runtime-evaluation-of-procedural-content-generation-in-an-endless-runner-game-using-autonomous-agents), which runs the validator in the same loop as the generator, and [§4.3](#43-from-unstable-to-playable-stabilizing-angry-birds-levels-via-object-segmentation), which repairs rather than regenerates — the block is coherent, and **it is the block the daily digests had been reporting as vacant.** The reason is categorical, not accidental: game PCG publishes to `cs.DS` / `cs.GR` / `cs.CY`, and §0.1 documents the category-scoping rule that would have caught it.

> **3. "Unsound but winning" is a recurring, under-reported result class.** [§1.1](#11-unsound-search-with-policy-and-value-networks-in-legends-of-code-and-magic) beats a no-search champion with deliberately unsound search (2^101 belief states, +24.6 points from search, and a *resistance* advantage against a best-response attack); [§1.2](#12-do-geometry-aware-positional-encodings-help-transformers-in-spatial-imperfect-information-games) improves belief cross-entropy while win rate goes *down* 1.56 pp. Both are honest, both are more useful than a clean result, and both arrive at the same methodological requirement: **report the closed-loop number next to the mechanism number, and run a best-response attack against your own system, not only the incumbent's.** The 09-28 window's [SPSD](../2026-09-28/game-rl-daily.md) (win rate 15% → 45% on a held-out game while transferring to maths, 24.1 → 36.6) is the constructive version of the same discipline.

> **4. Cost of a shipped game agent is dominated by retrieval substrate, not by the model.** [§3.4](#34-unity-insight-a-production-codeasset-index-for-llm-coding-agents-in-unity-projects) measures **53% fewer tokens and 52% less wall-clock** from a typed index, with the diagnosis that the coupling a gameplay change depends on lives in **`.meta` files and YAML, not in code**, so every code-only index is blind to it. [§3.2](#32-whale-a-simple-recipe-for-joint-harness-weight-optimization) finds the harness and weights bottleneck each other, that harness search alone can match peak weight-only accuracy in some domains, and that **interleaving beats staging**. [§6.3](#63-flashvector-agent-for-hierarchical-model-serving-stack-optimization) reports that in a production serving stack, **the wins came from the Python feature service and the server C++ rather than from the GPU kernel**. Three production-adjacent measurements converging on **"the layer you did not instrument is the one costing you."**

> **5. Player-facing AI is a trust problem before it is a capability problem.** [§6.4](#64-oops-not-now-pearl-a-rag-based-support-agent-for-gameplay-and-what-players-want-from-ai-help) finds **5 of 10 players abandoned the tool mid-play** and attributes it to proactive delivery, generic responses and trust deficits — the tool works *when the player initiates it in a specific state*. [§5.3](#53-detecting-ai-impostors-how-do-middle-schoolers-identify-llm-agents-in-a-live-collaborative-setting) finds detection accuracy improves by shifting **from linguistic cues to shared social context**, which implies an agent's **cross-session consistency** matters more than its turn-level fluency. [§6.1](#61-shut-up-and-let-me-enjoy-my-otome-understanding-and-measuring-the-toxicity-in-otome-game-communities) provides the population these agents land in: **22.20% vs 3.71% cross-platform toxicity, 37.09% within 72 hours of an external attack, 64.40% of coordination clusters targeting developers.** The composite implication: **ship the companion as something a player opts into, in a specific state, with consistent memory, and with a moderation policy tuned per platform — and budget for response latency during an incident, not just for detection.**
