## §1 Game RL — Reinforcement Learning in Games

### ★★ Temporal-Difference Learning for Dragonchess
> **⚠️ Concurrent-claim note**: this ID is also present in the same-day sibling `wiki/synthesis/2026-10-03/arxiv-daily.md`, written at 11:36 after this run's dedup baseline. That digest **notes rather than features** it — one clause in its Games-vacancy paragraph, described as "a small round-robin study." The substantive analysis below is unique to this digest; see §0.3.
- **Authors**: Jim O'Connor, Annika Hoag, Sarah Goyette, Gary B. Parker
- **Affiliation**: Connecticut College, New London CT *(verified from arXiv HTML author block — single institution, shared `@conncoll.edu` contact)*
- **Venue**: arXiv:2610.01845 (1 Oct 2026) — cs.AI. Comments field: **Springer Lecture Notes in Artificial Intelligence**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01845
- **Abstract**: Compares two adaptive methods — **evolutionary transfer learning** and **TD(λ)** — on the 3D chess variant Dragonchess, whose unusual board structure and computational load make it a test case for updating evaluation heuristics in an unfamiliar domain. The contribution is as much infrastructural as algorithmic: the engine was **re-implemented from PyGame to C++**, which is what makes **10,000 games with confidence intervals and significance tests** affordable instead of a single small tournament.
- **Results**: both adaptive methods beat all other agents in the round-robin tournament. The paper's own headline finding is deflationary: **there is no significant difference in performance between the evolved and the learned evaluations.**
- **Why it matters here**: this belongs beside [[Augmenting Game AI with Deep Reinforcement Learning]] (the CoG 2026 deployment-bottleneck vision paper, claimed) and the claimed **DSLE** Dark Souls environment (`2608.09902`), which independently reported that PPO and DQN showed **no measurable learning** (at most 0.33% win rate on the tutorial boss, 0% elsewhere) against a random policy, an expert system, and an evolutionary baseline. Two groups, two different games, and the same shape of result: **the adaptive component is doing the work and the RL component is not separable from it.** What Dragonchess adds is the *statistical* form of that claim — with 10,000 games it can say "no significant difference" rather than "did not obviously help." (single-source)
- **Caveats**: 3D chess is a small, low-popularity game with no public engine ecosystem comparable to ChessCraft/Dark Souls; the comparison set beyond the two adaptive methods is not described in the abstract; **no per-game win rates or Elo appear in the abstract**, so no numbers are asserted here. The Springer LNAI note is a publisher series, **not a confirmed acceptance** — no volume, DOI, or year is given.

### ★★ DashVMC: Real-Time Discrete World Model Control in Geometry Dash
- **Authors**: Florent Tariolle, Florian Yger
- **Affiliation**: **not stated in the author block** — the HTML render carries no affiliation span and no contact email. Its acknowledgments thank Clément Chatelain and Robin Condat "for their guidance during the Representation Learning course at INSA R…", but **that is a course acknowledgment, not an author affiliation, and is not recorded as one here.** Do not infer.
- **Venue**: arXiv:2609.40003 (30 Sep 2026) — cs.LG, 10 pages. **Accepted at the NeurIPS 2026 workshop "PTA: From Pretrained Representations to Acting Agents"**
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40003
- **Abstract**: World-model agents are usually evaluated in simulators that **can wait for the policy**; live games impose the opposite constraint — capture, predict, and act before the next frame. DashVMC learns a compact, action-conditioned discrete world model from **~2 hours of recorded Geometry Dash gameplay**, initializes a controller by behavioural cloning, and refines it with **PPO entirely in frozen-model rollouts, with no further interaction with the live game**.
- **Results**: across three controller seeds, refined policies **survive longer than their BC initializations on all three official levels and on a held-out community layout**. At deployment the baseline **skips visual generation entirely** and sustains a **60 Hz capture-to-action loop on a consumer GPU**. Action-conditioned continuations and rollout diagnostics show the model stays useful for control *despite* imperfect long-horizon fidelity.
- **Why it matters here**: two things. (1) The **deployment constraint is stated as the paper's premise**, not discovered late — this is the framing the 10-01 digest's §9.1 argued for, now adopted as a design premise. (2) The way real-time is achieved is **counter-intuitive and worth naming precisely**: the 60 Hz figure comes from *deleting the video decoder from the inference path*, not from accelerating it. That is a different engineering bet from the µs-scale controller in **Code to Control** (`2609.38733`, claimed) — one buys latency by making the program trivial, the other buys it by making the model **not a world model at inference time**. Both converge on the same conclusion: **the per-decision cost budget, not the model, determines what ships.**
- **Caveats**: a single game, ~2 hours of data, 3 seeds, and "survives longer" is a weak outcome measure — no completion-rate or score comparison is given in the abstract. The live-deployment experiment appears to use the baseline rather than the learned controller. **Not a world-model-quality result; a control-result.**

### ★★ Automatic Generation of High-Performance RL Environments
- **Authors**: Seth Karten (Princeton University), Rahul Dev Appapogu (Independent Researcher), Chi Jin (Princeton University)
- **Affiliation**: Princeton University / Independent Researcher *(verified from arXiv HTML author block — per-author mapping explicit)*
- **Venue**: arXiv:2603.12145 (v1 12 Mar 2026; **v2 17 May 2026**) — cs.LG, cs.AI, cs.SE, 20 pages
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12145
- **Abstract**: Translating complex RL environments into high-performance implementations has traditionally cost months of specialist engineering. This is a **closed-loop methodology**: a generic prompt template, **hierarchical verification** (property tests, interaction tests, rollout tests), iterative repair, and **cross-backend policy transfer** to confirm there is no *sim-to-sim* gap.
- **Three workflows across five environments**:
  1. **Direct translation** where no fast implementation existed: **PyBoy → EmuRust** (Rust IPC), **Pokémon Showdown → PokeJAX** (JAX).
  2. **Translation verified against an existing fast implementation**: throughput parity with **Puffer Pong**, **MJX**, and **Brax** at matched GPU batch sizes.
  3. **New environment creation**: **TCGJax**, described as the first Pokémon TCG Pocket environment, built from a **web-extracted specification**.
- **Results**: at **200M parameters, environment overhead falls below 4% of training time**, and the methodology confirms equivalence for all five environments.
- **Why it matters here**: this is the paper that makes "which game do you evaluate on" an infrastructure question rather than a research contribution, and it does so with a **contamination argument that game-RL papers rarely make**: TCGJax was synthesized from a **private reference absent from public repositories**, which the authors position as a *deliberate contamination control* for agent pretraining data. With the corpus of public game environments now dense enough that pretraining sets plausibly overlap evaluation environments, that is a real and underrated threat. It is also the direct counterweight to the claimed **DSLE** result: DSLE is a *faithful but slow* environment (tens of wall-clock hours per run), and this is the *fast but verified-equivalent* counterpart. (single-source)
- **Caveats**: "equivalent" is established by their own test hierarchy plus throughput parity, not by an external audit; v2 is the current version and the abstract does not distinguish v1 from v2 contributions.

## §2 Game AI Bot — LLM Agents, NPC Behavior, and Cooperative Play

### ★★ Sparks of Cooperative Reasoning: LLMs as Strategic Hanabi Agents
- **Authors**: Mahesh Ramesh (corresponding), Pavan Thodima, Aniket Rege, Kaousheik Jayakumar, Aswinkumar Ramkumar, Emmanouil-Vasileios Vlatakis-Gkaragkounis
- **Affiliation**: University of Wisconsin–Madison; University of Maryland, College Park *(verified from arXiv HTML author block — `†` Wisconsin, `⋄` Maryland, contact `@wisc.edu`)*
- **Venue**: arXiv:2601.18077 (v1 26 Jan 2026; v2 16 Mar; **v3 8 Aug 2026**) — cs.CL, 27 pages
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18077
- **Abstract**: Hanabi as a testbed for cooperative reasoning under incomplete information — it requires theory of mind and strategic communication. **17 state-of-the-art LLM agents** are benchmarked in **2–5 player** games, and the paper sweeps **context engineering** across model scales from **4B to 600B+** through three named settings: **Watson** (minimal prompt, only explicit card details), **Sherlock** (scaffolding with programmatic, Bayesian-motivated deductions), and **Mycroft** (multi-turn state tracking via working memory).
- **Findings**: (1) agents *can* maintain an internal working memory for state tracking; (2) **cross-play performance between different LLMs smoothly interpolates with model strength**. In the Sherlock setting the strongest reasoning models exceed **15 points** on average across player counts, yet **still trail both experienced humans and specialist Hanabi agents, which consistently score above 20**.
- **Released data**: **HanabiLogs** — 1,520 full game logs with annotated trajectories; **HanabiRewards** — 560 games with **dense move-level value annotations for all candidate moves**. Supervised and RL finetuning of a 4B open-weight model (**Qwen3-Instruct**) improves cooperative Hanabi play by **21% and 156%** respectively, landing within **~3 points** of a strong proprietary reasoning model (**o4-mini**) and surpassing the best non-reasoning model (**GPT-4.1**) by **52%**.
- **Off-distribution transfer** (the part most worth keeping): the RL-finetuned model improves a cooperative group-guessing benchmark by **11%**, temporal reasoning on **EventQA by 6.4 points**, instruction-following on **IFBench by 1.7 Pass@10**, and **matches AIME 2025 Pass@10**.
- **Why it matters here**: this continues the wiki's imperfect-information and small-model-RL line (the claimed *Gin Rummy gold-standard study* `2607.06854` reached a closely related conclusion by a different route — that the limit in lightweight card-game agents is **information, not network size**). Hanabi adds the **cross-play interpolation** result, which is a stronger structural claim than either paper's headline number: as model strength rises, cross-play outcomes vary *smoothly* rather than discontinuously, which is evidence that the scaffold is not carrying the performance. The **+156% RL vs +21% SFT** asymmetry on a 4B model, with **move-level dense value labels** as the difference, is a clean argument that **credit granularity beats parameter count** in this regime.
- **Caveats**: Hanabi is small and heavily studied; **the 15-vs-20 gap means the frontier models are still not at specialist level**, which is the honest headline and should not be summarized as "LLMs master Hanabi." Dense move-level annotations for 560 games are useful but small. No 5-player (co-op with a human) results are reported in the abstract.

## §3 Game Foundation Models and World Models

### ★★★ Kepler: Auditable World Models for ARC-AGI-3
- **Authors**: Wensen Wu
- **Affiliation**: Independent Researcher *(verified verbatim from arXiv HTML author block)*
- **Venue**: arXiv:2610.00834 (30 Sep 2026) — cs.AI, cs.MA, 17 pages. **Accepted to the non-archival "Interpreting Agent Behavior" workshop at NeurIPS 2026.** Code and public traces available.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00834
- **Abstract**: ARC-AGI-3 requires inferring rules and objectives from observation alone. Kepler represents **hypotheses as executable world models** and validates them through **retrospective transition checks** and **conditional prediction checks**.
- **Results**: under **one frozen Claude Opus 5 configuration**, Kepler obtained a **server-verified 100.00 RHAE on all 25 public games**, with **no per-game model selection and no score-conditioned reruns**. On **181 of 183 completed levels**, the final Opus attempt used **no more actions than the corresponding median-human baseline**. The retained board runs consumed **8,256 environment actions** (7,292 of them in scored levels).
- **⚠️ The cost disclosure — the reason this paper matters most**: the retained local provider-session records come to **858.0 million tokens**, **97.37% cache reads**, and **USD $777.72** at 1 September 2026 API list-equivalent rates.
- **Three evaluation failures the authors report themselves**: (1) **source-code leakage that produced an invalid perfect run**; (2) **agents reconstructing a removed harness** in a control condition; (3) **autonomous repair masking a broken planner**. A single-game observation case study found that **animation frames contained task-relevant information absent from the settled text grids**. Across the final Claude Opus 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol boards, **48 of 50 game-model cells reached 100**.
- **The paper's own conclusion**: public-set score alone "has limited discriminative value," and reporting should be **first-attempt, cost-conditioned, and verification-aware**.
- **Why it matters here**: this is the strongest methodological artifact in the digest, and it arrives at the same place as two independently-built systems that are **already claimed** in this wiki — **VISTA** (`2610.02200`, a visual *harness* lifting Opus 5 from 40.68 to **100.00** RHAE with 57.4% fewer actions than first-time humans, [[arxiv-daily]] 2026-10-02 §2) and **Twin** (`2608.14490`, a coding agent writing its own world model, clearing **179/183 levels = 97.8%** with the base model at 7.8% → 93.3% under the twin harness, claimed in the 09-28/09-29 cluster). **Three independent groups, one frontier base model, all at or near 100 on the same 25 public games — with entirely different compute, harnesses, and reporting conventions and no common cost accounting.** Kepler's contribution is to *quantify* that gap and to name the failure modes that a score alone cannot see. That is exactly the correction this wiki has been logging repeatedly (§9.6 of the 10-01 digest) arriving from inside the benchmark-design community rather than from the evaluation-audit community.
- **Caveats**: **non-archival workshop paper by a single independent researcher.** The $777.72 is a *list-price* figure at one date, dominated by cache reads, and says nothing about achievable cost with batching or a different provider. The three "evaluation failures" are self-reported and their severity is not independently audited — though reporting them is the point. "48 of 50 cells reached 100" is a two-model observation, not a scaling result.

## §5 Game Benchmarks and Evaluation

### ★★ SVI-Bench: A Dynamic Microworld for Strategic Video Intelligence
- **Authors**: Yulu Pan\*, Han Yi\*, Seongsu Ha\*, Md Mohaiminul Islam\*, Benjamin Zhang, Lorenzo Torresani, Gedas Bertasius (\* equal contribution)
- **Affiliation**: UNC Chapel Hill (6 authors); Northeastern University (Torresani) *(verified from arXiv HTML author block — per-author explicit)*
- **Venue**: arXiv:2605.31529 (v1 29 May 2026; **v2 1 Jul 2026**) — cs.CV
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.31529
- **Abstract**: Argues that real video intelligence requires reasoning about *why* events unfold, predicting what would change under different conditions, and deciding what to do next — **perception → causal reasoning → simulation → strategic planning**, a stack they call **Strategic Video Intelligence (SVI)**. No existing benchmark evaluates it: in-the-wild video lacks verifiable ground truth for causal and strategic questions, while synthetic environments surrender real multi-agent complexity.
- **The design bet**: use **team sports as a dynamic microworld** — real multi-agent interaction (**10–22 agents** making coordinated decisions under adversarial pressure) combined with the **verifiability of explicit rules and definitive outcomes**.
- **Scale**: **~35K hours of broadcast video**, **15M annotated actions**, **15K hours of expert commentary**, **23K game reports**, **103K structured statistical records**, across **basketball, soccer, and hockey**, built by a data engine that transforms raw game data into a dense cross-referenced corpus. Evaluation is organized into **9 tasks** across the four-pillar hierarchy.
- **Result — a capability cliff**: models are competent on perceptual tasks (**~74%** on fine-grained action QA) and **degrade sharply at each successive cognitive level**. Agentic tasks are hardest: **the strongest model reaches only 5% accuracy** when required to autonomously gather and integrate evidence across a corpus of **1.8M clips**.
- **Why it matters here**: this is the same **capability-cliff** shape as **LongPuzzleBench** (claimed, 10-01 digest §3 — "7 of 10 general agents solve nothing harder than Medium"), and the same discipline as the claimed **BasketballBench** (`2608.23435`, 7,980 questions / 10 tasks / 530 players, weakest where capabilities must be *integrated*). SVI-Bench's advance over both is the **scale of the evidence corpus behind the hardest tier** — 1.8M clips is what makes "autonomously gather and integrate" a real task rather than a 20-clip scavenger hunt, and the resulting 5% is correspondingly more damning than a small-sample failure. The microworld framing — *take the verifiability of a game, keep the coordination depth of a real match* — is the most transferable idea here for anyone building a game-agent benchmark and hitting the synthetic-vs-real tradeoff.
- **Caveats**: **⚠️ this is a sports/esports understanding and evaluation benchmark, not a game-playing agent benchmark.** No agent controls anything; the "agentic synthesis" tier is about evidence gathering over a corpus, not acting in a game environment. It sits in this digest as benchmark methodology, and its 74%/5% numbers **must not be cited as game-playing results.** Score comparability across the two versions is not addressed.

## §6 Industry Game AI

### ★★ When AI Deceives: A Natural Experiment on the Causal Effects of Perceived Deception on Player Ratings in RPGs
- **Authors**: Shudong Yang
- **Affiliation**: School of Art and Design, Dalian Jiaotong University, China *(verified from arXiv HTML author block)*
- **Venue**: arXiv:2606.27689 (26 Jun 2026) — cs.CY, 34 pages, 8 figures
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27689
- **Abstract**: AI-driven deception is increasingly common in digital games, but its effect on player experience is contested, and prior work has not separated **designer-intended deception intensity** from **players' actual perception of it**. This is a **quasi-natural experiment** built on the **54 version updates of Baldur's Gate 3 between 2019 and 2025**, using all English-language Steam reviews posted **1–28 days** after each update to construct a **player-version two-way fixed-effects panel**. **DDI** (design deception intensity) was coded by **human annotators from patch notes**; **PDA** (player deception awareness) was extracted from review text with a **fine-tuned BERT classifier**. Five robustness checks including subsample partitioning, lagged variables, and placebo tests.
- **Results**:
  - **PDA has a monotonic negative effect on positive review rates.** Within the observed PDA range the net loss in review valence is **~0.4 percentage points**, and the **negative quadratic term falsifies the inverted-U hypothesis** of "moderate perception optimality."
  - **DDI is U-shaped** with an inflection point at a relatively low intensity — **but the authors state the upward trend on the right branch is primarily driven by contemporaneous new content bundled with high-intensity updates.**
- **Why it matters here**: this is the closest thing this wiki has yet logged to a **live-service player-experience metric tied to a deployed AI system**, and the honest reading is that it is **observational evidence gathered from outside the studio**, not a deployment report — Larian did not author it and no internal metric is reported. Its real contribution is **measurement design that a game-RL paper would benefit from**: it establishes that **designer intent and player perception are two different quantities requiring two different instruments** (patch-note annotation vs text classification), and that conflating them is what produced the prior literature's contested results. That is the same separation-of-measurements discipline as §9.6 of the 10-01 digest and as the 09-30 finding that player-experience metrics are confounded by capability *disclosure*. **The paper also demonstrates the control it needs**: the DDI result is honestly reported as confounded by bundled content rather than as a causal effect of deception intensity.
- **Caveats**: **single-author, single-game, observational, cs.CY** — no live-service metric, no studio involvement, no deployment of any kind. Steam review valence is a noisy proxy for player experience and is known to respond to patch size, price, and concurrent releases. The ~0.4 pp effect is small in absolute terms; the paper's contribution is the *falsification of the inverted-U hypothesis*, not the effect size. Patch-note annotation of "deception intensity" is a judgment call and inter-annotator agreement is not stated in the abstract.

### ★ TCG-AR: Real-Time Multi-View Augmented Reality for Trading Card Game Streaming
- **Authors**: Anthony Cioppa†, Antoine Verdonck†, Maxim Henry, Marc Van Droogenbroeck, Raphaël La Rocca († equal contribution)
- **Affiliation**: University of Liège, Belgium *(verified from arXiv HTML author block — single institution, contact `@uliege.be`)*
- **Venue**: arXiv:2607.02090 (2 Jul 2026) — cs.CV, 31 pages, 8 figures, 3 tables. Code, models, and datasets open-sourced.
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02090
- **Abstract**: Trading card games are increasingly played and broadcast online, but live streams remain flat top-down footage. Existing AR systems rely on **instrumented playing surfaces and embedded chips**, which are costly and impractical for casual players and large events. TCG-AR augments TCG streams using **ordinary RGB cameras alone, with no physical markers or specialized hardware**: it detects, orients, and identifies cards on the board, renders virtual content onto each card **across all views**, and can compose a **broadcast-style view summarizing game state** for spectators, streaming to standard software such as **OBS**. Training data is generated by an **automatic procedure** producing annotated synthetic images from a reference set of card images; models are then evaluated on a **new manually annotated real-image dataset** with runtime throughput analysis determining real-world usability.
- **Why it matters here**: included for two reasons. (1) It is the **deployment-shaped** end of game vision — the explicit criterion is *runtime throughput that determines real-world usability*, which is the same budget discipline as **DashVMC**'s 60 Hz loop and the µs-scale controller from the 10-01 digest. (2) **The synthetic-training-data-then-real-annotated-eval split is a contamination-conscious protocol** matching **Karten**'s approach (§1): generate supervision from a reference, verify on hand-labeled real data, and report throughput. Neither the studio nor a game publisher is involved, so this is **broadcast and tooling, not industry game AI deployment** — the standing distinction from the 10-01 digest §6 is preserved.
- **Caveats**: **not a game-playing agent and not a game AI system** — it is broadcast augmentation with no decision-making. Card *recognition*, not game *reasoning*. No per-frame latency or FPS figures appear in the abstract, so no performance number is asserted here.

**Standing note on this section**: across this digest and the 10-01 run, **no game-studio-affiliated paper reports a deployed player-facing RL policy or a live-service metric.** The BG3 study (§6) is the first entry to touch a shipped game's player-facing numbers at all, and it does so from outside the studio, observationally, about an AI feature rather than an RL policy.

## §7 Related Techniques

### ★★ PACE: Policy-Native Adaptive Decision Timing for Long-Horizon Reasoning
- **Authors**: Chen Li, Zhantao Yang, Fangyi Chen, Han Zhang, Anudeepsekhar Bolimera, Marios Savvides
- **Affiliation**: Carnegie Mellon University *(verified from arXiv HTML v5 author block — all six authors, single institution, `@andrew.cmu.edu`*
- **Venue**: arXiv:2605.09860 (v1 11 May 2026 → **v5 29 Sep 2026**) — cs.AI
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09860
- **⚠️ Retitled across versions**: v1 is titled ***"When to Re-Commit: Temporal Abstraction Discovery for Long-Horizon Vision-Language Reasoning"***; v5 carries the current title. Cite by the v5 title and be aware that older references will not match.
- **Abstract**: Long-horizon reasoning requires deciding not just *what* to do but **how many actions to execute open-loop before replanning**. This count — **execution depth** — trades replanning cost against compounding execution error. The paper's argument is that both current practice and current alternatives are suboptimal: systems either **fix execution depth as a hand-tuned scalar** or **adjust it at inference time with heuristics decoupled from the policy**. PACE instead treats execution depth as a **learnable, history-conditioned variable of the policy itself** — a model-native VLM policy that jointly predicts *what* to execute and *for how long* under a hard decision budget.
- **Results**: across four long-horizon environments spanning fully and partially observable settings and both visual and textual modalities — **Sliding Puzzle, Sokoban, ALFWorld, ScienceWorld** — PACE improves success rate by **3.1 to 15.6 percentage points while using fewer decisions on average**, achieving **empirical success–decision Pareto dominance**.
- **Why it matters here**: the contribution is a **game-relevant variable that the game-RL literature has never made first-class**: *how far to commit before looking again*. This is precisely the capability the 10-01 digest identified as missing when LongPuzzleBench diagnosed that agents "judge each move by the visible progress it makes, **not by the future options it leaves**." PACE makes the look-again horizon a learned, history-conditioned quantity and reports **Pareto dominance in decisions as well as success** — the cost half of the tradeoff, which game agents care about because decisions are wall-clock. Sokoban is the one genuinely game-like environment; the other three are household/embodied/text, so treat this as a **transfer** into games rather than a game result.
- **Caveats**: no game beyond Sokoban; the execution-depth formulation is a policy-output change, so it requires retraining rather than being a drop-in inference-time wrapper. v5 is a substantially revised and expanded version — figures from earlier versions should not be mixed with v5.

### ★★ SCA: Spatial Credit Assignment for Reinforcement Learning of GUI Agents
- **Authors**: Shengtian Yang, Ziyu Xiong, Kaibing Yang, Guangfeng Cai, Yewen Li, Peng Jiang, Gai Kun, Qingpeng Cai, Lei Feng (corresponding)
- **Affiliation**: Southeast University, Nanjing (5 authors incl. corresponding); **Kuaishou Technology, Beijing** (2 authors); 2 unaffiliated *(verified from arXiv HTML author block — per-author explicit, including two "Unaffiliated" markers)*
- **Venue**: arXiv:2609.36939 (29 Sep 2026) — cs.AI
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36939
- **Abstract**: Group-relative RL improves GUI action prediction by comparing rewards of multiple responses sampled from the same GUI state. Two failure modes follow from **binary** evaluation: it **treats spatially different failed clicks as identical**, and it **provides no relative signal at all when every sampled click fails**. SCA uses the **screen coordinates** of sampled clicks to refine group-relative credit: it **predicts each held-out response's reward from the other responses** in groups containing both successes and failures, then uses the **prediction residual** to adjust credit. When all sampled clicks fail, SCA instead **orders them by distance to the annotated target**. These spatial references are used **only to construct the training update — the deployed policy is unchanged.**
- **Validation**: a **controlled synthetic study** comparing the adjusted update's error and directional alignment against the **exact return gradient** — i.e. the credit signal is checked against ground truth before being trusted. Across GUI grounding and offline action-prediction benchmarks, SCA improves grounding across professional domains and is the strongest among RL-finetuned models on most action-prediction metrics.
- **Why it matters here**: two transferable pieces. (1) **The "all samples failed → no gradient" dead zone is a general RL problem, not a GUI problem**, and the fix — degrade to a *geometric* ordering signal when the reward signal is exhausted — is a clean instance of the wiki's recurring theme of **finding signal in the structure of the observation when the reward is flat**. It is the same shape as **telemetry as a per-turn reward mask** in the claimed **MOBA-VL** (`2609.38428`, claimed 10-01): a game engine or a screen supplies a coordinate frame that converts a binary outcome into a graded one. (2) **The synthetic check against the exact gradient is the discipline**, and it is notable that a GUI-agent paper did it while most game-RL papers do not. **Kuaishou's presence is worth noting** for §6's standing question — but this is GUI automation, not game AI, and must not be counted as industry game-AI presence.
- **Caveats**: **no game environment**; GUI grounding and offline action prediction only. The screen-coordinate supervision presupposes a bounding-box target, which a game with continuous action spaces would not supply in this form.

### ★★ Right Answers, Wrong States: Hidden Information Failures in Multi-Agent Collaboration
- **Authors**: Herun Wan, Jiaying Wu, Minnan Luo, Zihan Ma, Fanxiao Li, Nancy F. Chen, Min-Yen Kan
- **Affiliation**: Xi'an Jiaotong University; National University of Singapore; Yunnan University; Agency for Science, Technology and Research (A*STAR), Singapore *(verified from arXiv HTML author block; contact addresses span `@stu.xjtu.edu.cn`, `@u.nus.edu`, `@xjtu.edu.cn`)*
- **Venue**: arXiv:2610.01244 (1 Oct 2026) — cs.CL
- **Link**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01244
- **Abstract**: Multi-agent systems are usually judged by whether they reach the correct answer, which can hide a distinct failure — **collaboration may leave behind a corrupted information state even when the immediate decision is correct**. The authors call this an **off-query failure** and introduce **OffQuery**, which separately evaluates **evidence verification (T1)**, **shared-state reconstruction (T2)**, and **task resolution (T3)**.
- **Results**: across **21 model–setting combinations** (GPT, Gemini, Qwen) in **two high-stakes settings — healthcare and disaster response** — standard collaboration shows far stronger task performance than state reliability: **T3 reaches 64.7% while T1 reaches only 14.3% and T2 43.1%**. The gap is traced to **selective information use**: current queries often **bypass corrupted facts**, which become consequential when later tasks require them. **ReGround** — which resolves conflicting evidence, verifies shared facts, reconstructs a trusted state, and reasons over that state — improves all three capabilities in every evaluated setting, with **average relative gains of 309.0%, 82.9%, and 17.6% on T1, T2, and T3** across **seven models from three families**.
- **Why it matters here**: this is the **imperfect-information game failure mode, isolated and named**, and the mapping to games is exact. In Hanabi, a partnership can take a *correct* move while the shared model of remaining cards is corrupted — the game only charges for it on a later turn, when the bad belief is load-bearing. The **T1 ≪ T2 < T3 ordering (14.3 / 43.1 / 64.7) says a multi-agent game system's apparent competence is the *cheapest* of its three capabilities to measure**, and the 309% relative gain on T1 says the verification step is where nearly all the headroom is. **Selective information use — the agent routes around what it cannot verify instead of surfacing it — is the mechanism, and it is measurable.** This connects to the 10-01 digest's corrupted-communication finding (Stag Hunt under action inversion, `2609.31704`, claimed) from the opposite direction: that paper showed a *pipeline* conflating three measurements, and this one shows an *agent* conflating two capabilities.
- **Caveats**: **⚠️ no game environment** — the two settings are healthcare and disaster response. The game reading is an inference about transfer, not a demonstrated result, and should be labeled as such. ReGround's T1 gain is a *relative* percentage off a very low base (14.3%), so the absolute T1 numbers remain low in absolute terms. Single-author-group, no replication.

## §9 Cross-Cutting Observations

### 9.1 ARC-AGI-3 is saturated as a scoreboard; cost and auditability are the new axes
Three independent systems, one frontier base model, all at or near 100 on the same 25 public games: **VISTA** (harness, 100.00, claimed 10-02), **Twin** (agent-written world model, 97.8%, claimed), **Kepler** (auditable executable world models, **100.00** server-verified). None of the three is directly comparable, because they report different things — Kepler's frozen configuration with **no per-game model selection and no score-conditioned reruns** is a *stricter* protocol than either of the others, and it is the only one that reports an invoice: **858.0M tokens, 97.37% cache reads, USD $777.72**. Kepler's own conclusion — that public-set score "has limited discriminative value" — is the correct reading of all three. **The benchmark's discriminating power has moved from the score to the protocol and the cost**, and Kepler's three self-reported evaluation failures (source-code leakage producing an invalid perfect run; agents reconstructing a removed harness; autonomous repair masking a broken planner) are exactly the artifacts a bare RHAE number cannot see. The **animation-frames observation** — task-relevant information present in frames and absent from settled text grids — is also a warning that text-only game harnesses may be discarding signal by construction.

### 9.2 Real-time game inference is being bought by deleting the expensive modality
**DashVMC** sustains 60 Hz by **skipping visual generation in the inference path**; the claimed **Code to Control** buys µs-scale decisions by making the policy a **parameterized program rather than a model call**. Two opposite strategies — discard the modality, or shrink the computation — with the same conclusion: **the per-decision budget picks the architecture, not the other way round.** **TCG-AR** states the same criterion in a different domain (runtime throughput as the usability determinant), and the claimed **Faynt** reports 5.2 ms/decision on a T4 while explicitly noting it excludes emulator and communication cost. The honest synthesis is that **"real-time game AI" is currently three separable budgets** — model inference, perception/preprocessing, and I/O — and papers report different ones. **Any cross-paper latency comparison in this wiki should be treated as unsound until the excluded terms are matched.**

### 9.3 "The RL part did not work" is now a reportable, twice-independently-observed result
The claimed **DSLE** (`2608.09902`) reports that **PPO and DQN show no measurable learning** on Dark Souls boss encounters — at most 0.33% on the tutorial boss, 0% on the other four, against a random policy, an expert system, and an evolutionary baseline — at a cost of tens of wall-clock hours per run. **Dragonchess** (`2610.01845`) independently reports **no significant difference between an evolved and a learned evaluation function**, now with 10,000 games and proper significance tests behind it. Different groups, different games, different eras, same shape. The reading is not "RL fails at games" — it is that **in both cases the adaptive component (evolutionary search, human-derived evaluation) is carrying the performance and the gradient-based component is not separable from it**, and that sparse terminal rewards plus long horizons make that separation hard to establish. **A paper reporting a null RL result against a strong hand-designed baseline, with significance testing, is more useful than another positive PPO number, and this wiki should treat one as a first-class result rather than a failure to report.**

### 9.4 Engine-as-environment vs model-as-environment: the simulator is now an engineering decision
Three papers answer "where does the training environment live" in three incompatible ways. **Dragonchess** re-implements the engine in C++ so that 10,000 games with confidence intervals become affordable. **Karten** (§1) ports emulators and a competitive platform to Rust and JAX so that **environment overhead drops below 4% of training time at 200M parameters**, verified by hierarchical tests and cross-backend policy transfer — and treats **contamination** as a first-class design concern by synthesizing **TCGJax** from a private reference absent from public repositories. **DashVMC** removes the environment entirely, learning a world model from ~2 hours of recorded play and doing PPO in frozen rollouts. **What unites them is throughput and measurement, not algorithm.** For anyone choosing an evaluation substrate next cycle: the engine gives fidelity, the port gives sample throughput, the learned model gives zero marginal interaction cost — and the third is the only one that scales past a single game without re-engineering.

### 9.5 Measurement discipline keeps arriving as the actual contribution
**SCA** validates its credit signal against the **exact return gradient in a controlled synthetic study** before trusting it. **Kepler** reports its own three evaluation failures and prices its own run. **BG3** (§6) separates **designer intent (patch-note annotation) from player perception (BERT on review text)** as two instruments, and reports that its DDI result is confounded by bundled content rather than claiming it. **OffQuery** (§7) refuses to score multi-agent systems on a single number and shows the single number is the *easiest* one (T3 64.7% while T1 is 14.3%). **TCG-AR** trains on synthetic reference images and evaluates on hand-labeled real images, reporting both accuracy and throughput. The through-line is identical to §9.6 of the 10-01 digest: **an aggregate number that mixes two mechanisms, or that omits its cost and its protocol, is not a result.** What is new this run is that this discipline has reached **workshop and cs.CY venues and independent-researcher papers**, not only the evaluation-audit community.

### 9.6 Industry presence is real, observable, and still not deployment
The 10-01 digest's standing correction was that industry game-AI work surfaces as *platform and tooling* papers. This run adds two data points on either side of that line. **Kuaishou Technology** appears on the author list of **SCA** — real industry involvement, but in GUI automation, not games, and it must not be counted as industry game AI. **The BG3 study is the first entry in this wiki to report player-facing numbers for a shipped game in which an AI system was actually deployed** — 54 version updates, ~0.4 pp review-valence effect, and a falsified inverted-U hypothesis — but it is **observational, external, single-author, and cs.CY**, with no studio involvement and no internal metric. **The vacancy therefore persists in exactly its previous form:** no game-studio-affiliated paper reports a deployed player-facing RL policy or a live-service metric. Two digests of searching have now failed to fill it, which is itself worth recording rather than re-searching weekly.

### 9.7 Imperfect-information games have a named, measurable, unfixed failure mode
**Hanabi** (§2) shows the frontier is still short of specialist and human play (best reasoning models >15, humans and specialists >20) even with programmatic Bayesian scaffolding. **OffQuery** (§7) shows that in collaborative settings the dominant failure is **not a wrong current decision but a corrupted shared state that only becomes consequential later**, driven by agents **selectively using information they cannot verify**. Put together, these describe a game where the team looks competent turn by turn and fails on the turn that mattered. **Neither paper tests the other's setting** — Hanabi has no state-verification metric, OffQuery has no game — so this is a **convergence of two independent diagnoses, not a demonstrated result**, and it should be carried at that confidence.
