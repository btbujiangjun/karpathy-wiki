### 1.1 Improved Accuracy Scaling with Problem Complexity in LLM Arithmetic Reasoning Using Executable Symbolic Representations
- **arXiv:** [2611.27203](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27203) [cs.LG] — 20 Nov 2026
- **Authors:** Yu-Bin Liu; Shi-Ju Ran
- **Institution/Company:** Not listed in arXiv metadata
- **Abstract:** High accuracy on mathematical benchmarks does not establish whether reasoning remains reliable as problem complexity increases. We examine how executable symbolic representations change accuracy scaling in large language models compared with natural-language reasoning. To address this limitation, we introduce the GSM Arithmetic Benchmark (GSM-AB), a controlled benchmark that independently varies operand digit length and operation count to probe numerical scale and compositional complexity. We find that executable representations can improve accuracy and slow its decline as complexity increases. Lean reduces average sensitivity to operand digit length at every tested model size. By contrast, slower degradation with increasing operation count emerges at the larger model sizes. A Python program-of-thought (Python-PoT) control shows a similar operation-axis pattern, suggesting that this finding is not specific to Lean. These findings show that reasoning representation shapes arithmetic accuracy scaling and support executable symbolic representations for more reliable reasoning on complex arithmetic tasks.
- **Key innovations:**

  - Executable symbolic representations (Lean, Python-PoT) improve accuracy and slow its decline as complexity increases; Lean reduces average sensitivity to operand digit length at every tested model size.

### 1.4 Markets for Compute and Inference: Structure, Financing, and Risk Transfer
- **arXiv:** [2611.20242](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20242) [q-fin.CP; cs.AI] — 15 Nov 2026
- **Authors:** Dr Gabriel Borrageiro
- **Institution/Company:** Not listed in arXiv metadata
- **Abstract:** Capacity to run large language models is rented by the accelerator-hour, and their output is sold by the million tokens. Both goods now have published price indices, an over-the-counter forward has traded on one, compute futures are listed offshore, and CME and ICE have specified futures on rental indices that await the United States regulator. We ask what in these two markets can be hedged, by whom, and at what price. Three findings stand out. First, the price that exchange contracts settle on is not the price that drives operators' revenue. The contracts on CME and ICE reference published short-dated rental rates, while most revenue, and with it credit quality, comes from private multi-year contracts whose prices are largely undisclosed. Second, the token price and the rental rate are linked by an identity through throughput and margin, but they are different exposures, so a hedge on the rental rate does not hedge the cost of inference. Third, the demand for hedging already sits in credit agreements, as the years by which operators' debt outlives their contracted revenue. Filings cannot measure that gap, because debt is scheduled year by year while contracted revenue rarely is, and the one loan that prices it settles the risk by cash prepayment against a model only the parties see. We design a reverse convertible note through which leveraged operators could buy protection against falling rental rates. Its coupon depends steeply on the annualised volatility of the rental rate, which the two public indices put between about 5% and 89% according to how each is built and the horizon measured; across that range the fair one-year coupon runs from 4.1% to 31.4%.
- **Key innovations:**
  - Identifies a training/hedging gap: exchange-settled prices on CME/ICE reference short-dated rates, while most operator revenue comes from undisclosed multi-year private contracts.
  - Finds token price and rental rate are linked by a throughput/margin identity but different exposures — hedging rental rate does not hedge inference cost.
  - Demand for hedging sits in credit agreements (debt outlives contracted revenue); designs a reverse convertible note for leveraged operators; fair coupon ranges 4.1%–31.4% given rental rate volatility of 5%–89%.

### 1.5 Generating grain boundary structures at a specified energy with a symmetry-constrained diffusion model
- **arXiv:** [2611.27188](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27188) [cond-mat.mtrl-sci; physics.comp-ph] — 20 Nov 2026
- **Authors:** Kazuya Miyamoto; Ibuki Okuda; Izumi Takahara; Teruyasu Mizoguchi
- **Institution/Company:** Not listed in arXiv metadata
- **Abstract:** Grain boundaries (GBs) govern many properties of polycrystalline materials, and inverse design would make the GB structure itself a design variable. Crystal generative models such as MatterGen operate on periodic cells, which contain two GBs. The cell energy defines a single GB only when the two are equivalent, but existing models do not enforce this equivalence. Building on MatterGen, we developed a symmetry-constrained diffusion model in which the two GBs are related by a symmetry operation: atoms near one GB are generated, and those near the other are reconstructed through a symmetry projection applied at every denoising step. We applied it to the $Σ5(210)[001]$ symmetric tilt GB in Al, conditioning generation on the GB energy from a machine-learning interatomic potential (MLIP). All 350 generated structures kept a geometrically equivalent GB pair after relaxation. Given a target GB energy, the model generated structures at that energy: for the three in-range targets with many nearby training structures, 44% to 70% fell within ±0.1 J/m² of the target. Tracking weakened at higher energies and failed outside the training range. Of the 91 near-target structures, 90 matched no training structure under the adopted geometric criterion, indicating that the model can propose configurations near the target energy that are distinct from the training structures, a capability that could help identify unknown metastable GB structures observed in experiments. Building GB-pair equivalence into generation thus makes GB properties well-defined design targets, enabling inverse design of GB structures.
- **Key innovations:**
  - Symmetry-constrained diffusion model: two GBs in a periodic cell related by symmetry projection at every denoising step, enforcing geometric equivalence.
  - Conditions generation on GB energy (predicted by MLIP); all 350 generated structures kept equivalent GB pairs after relaxation.
  - 44–70% of in-range targets within ±0.1 J/m² of target; 90/91 near-target structures novel — supports inverse design of GB structures, including unknown metastable ones.

### 1.9 RegimeCouncil: Paired-Evidence Routing For Memory Conflict Governance In LLM Agents
- **arXiv:** [2611.22113](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22113) [cs.AI] — 17 Nov 2026
- **Authors:** Qingcan Kang; Mingyang Liu; Shixiong Kai; Tao Zhong; Mingxuan Yuan
- **Institution/Company:** Not listed in arXiv metadata
- **Abstract:** Alongside memory storage and retrieval, a fundamental challenge in long-term LLM agent systems is how the agent should act when stored memories conflict with an evolving world. Facts often go stale without explicit deletion, so the conflict surfaces before the store is refreshed: delayed feedback shows that acting on one outdated record hurt, but not whether suppression should stay on that fact (a local policy) or extend to other stored facts (a global policy). ### 1.10 Let the Gate Decide: Per-Lookup Precision for Quantized Conditional Memory in LLMs
- **arXiv:** [2610.35985](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35985) [cs.LG; cs.AI] — 26 Oct 2026
- **Authors:** Tianqi Chen; Xunchao Li; Linchuan Xie; Guanghua Yu; Jianchen Zhu; Peisong Wang; Jian Cheng
- **Institution/Company:** Not listed in arXiv metadata
- **Abstract:** Conditional memory layers, such as DeepSeek's Engram, attach large hashed n-gram tables to a language model, and removing them from DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash costs 36.8 PopQA points. Low-bit quantization loses long-tail facts first, and the standard remedy, giving more bits to frequently accessed rows, backfires: refining lookups by access frequency is worse than refining them at random. We trace this to the gate that scales each retrieved row. A lookup's quantization error is weighted by its gate, which is small for frequent n-grams, large for the rare n-grams of long-tail entities, and computable from a 1-bit code. GateRQ therefore stores each row as a ladder of 1-bit residual codes, keeps level 1 in GPU memory to compute the gate, and reads deeper levels only for the lookups the gate ranks highest. Without retraining, GateRQ cuts GPU table memory by 7.8x on DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, which raises vLLM throughput by 11%-31% over FP8 tables, while losing about 1 PopQA point at 1.50 bits read per lookup and 0.2 points at 2.10 bits across two models.
- **Key innovations:**
  - GateRQ: per-lookup precision for quantized conditional memory — 1-bit residual code ladder; gate computable from level-1 codes; deeper levels read only for top-ranked lookups.
  - 7.8× GPU table memory cut on DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash; vLLM throughput +11–31% over FP8 tables; ~1 PopQA point lost at 1.50 bits per lookup.

### 2.1 TS-GEAR: Taobao Shangou GEnerative Ad Retrieval

- **Abstract:** On-demand delivery platforms such as DoorDash and Uber Eats impose a constraint absent in traditional e-commerce: merchants beyond the user's delivery range are invalid candidates rather than merely low-ranked. ### 3.1 Variance-Optimal Counterfactual Policy Design for Sample-Efficient Online A/B Testing
- **arXiv:** [2611.24757](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24757) [cs.LG; cs.IR] — 18 Nov 2026
- **Authors:** Shashank Gupta; Subhadeep Maji
- **Institution/Company:** Not listed in arXiv metadata
- **Abstract:** Online A/B testing is the standard approach for evaluating changes to user-facing systems, such as a new recommendation or ranking policy, before deployment. While the difference-in-means estimator of a standard A/B test is unbiased, it often suffers from high variance. When the treatment and production policies choose the same actions with similar probabilities, the resulting observations can add noise while providing little information about the difference between the policies. Consequently, A/B tests can require large amounts of traffic and long durations to detect small treatment effects. Existing variance-reduction methods mainly improve the estimator after the data-collection scheme has been fixed, and recent overlap-aware designs only optimize a single traffic split between the two policies; thereby, they cannot direct traffic to the actions on which the policies disagree. In this paper, we jointly optimize how actions are sampled and how the resulting observations are used to estimate the treatment effect. Our contributions are two-fold. First, building on pairwise off-policy evaluation, we derive the variance-optimal action-level logging policy for estimating the average treatment effect between two policies, which has a closed-form solution for any fixed control variate. Second, we use historical logs to jointly optimize the logging policy and a control variate to further reduce variance. Our experimental results in simulated policy A/B tests show that our designs estimate the average treatment effect more accurately than existing variance reduction methods on every evaluated setting, while generally improving statistical power and reducing how often the worse policy is selected. As a result, our designs enable practitioners to reach reliable deployment decisions with considerably less online traffic.
- **Key innovations:**
  - Variance-optimal action-level logging policy with closed form (from pairwise off-policy evaluation) for estimating the average treatment effect; jointly optimizes the logging policy and a control variate using historical logs.
  - Simulated A/B tests: more accurate effect estimates on every setting, better statistical power, lower probability of selecting the worse policy.

### 4.1 Learning to Defer under Expert Drift

- **Authors:** Ziyao Mou; Andrea Wynn; Eric Nalisnot

- **Abstract:** The learning to defer (L2D) framework enables safety in machine learning and AI systems by allocating difficult or critical decisions to a human expert. Humans commonly exhibit such substantial, systematic performance shifts across a wide range of domains, from high-stakes settings such as radiology, aviation, and judicial decision-making, to everyday tasks such as crowdsourced image annotation. Our results show that EDA-L2D adapts more effectively than prior L2D methods to diverse temporal patterns of expert reliability, resulting in improved system performance and more effective deferral decisions.
- **Key innovations:**

In Test Case 2 (TC2), conducted in a different spatial configuration permitting bidirectional walking, lateral positions differed by stripe-motion direction during one-way flow, but coverage effects on lateral position and stripe-advertisement differences in lateral position or passing-side choice were not significant. In both test cases, partial stripes received lower self-reported sickness ratings than full-screen stripes. These findings suggest that lower-contrast and reduced-motion-coverage designs hold practical potential for pedestrian guidance, with spatially restricted cues potentially allowing such guidance to coexist with existing advertising while reducing discomfort. At the same time, the contrasting patterns in TC1 and TC2 suggest that spatial context may shape how pedestrians respond to stripe coverage, highlighting the context-dependent suitability of visual-motion-based guidance and the need for further investigation.
- **Key innovations:**
  - Two field studies (museum, 18,402 pedestrian passages over 25 days): partial stripe presentation → more leftward lateral positions; partial stripes → lower self-reported sickness than full-screen stripes.
  - Reduced-motion-coverage/lower-contrast stripe designs hold practical potential for guidance coexisting with existing signage/advertising while reducing discomfort; context dependence between one-way (TC1) and bidirectional (TC2) flow.

### 7.6 SkillPrivus: Uncovering PRIVacy of USe Harms in Agentic Skills
- **arXiv:** [2611.26747](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26747) [cs.CL] — 19 Nov 2026
- **Authors:** Mariana Fernandez-Espinosa; Ivoline Ngong; Keerthiram Murugesan; Karthikeyan Natesan Ramamurthy
- **Institution/Company:** Not listed in arXiv metadata
- **Abstract:** Agent skills extend AI assistants through installable instructions and code that can access users' files, accounts, and data. Even when performing their advertised tasks, skills may infer sensitive attributes, retain unnecessary information, or disclose data to unexpected recipients. We study these potential privacy-of-use harms through SkillPrivus, a static audit framework that analyzes skills before installation without executing them. SkillPrivus extracts data operations from code and natural-language instructions, grounds them in source evidence, and adapts Contextual Integrity to assess their appropriateness. Applied to 726 real skills from public marketplaces, 563 of which handle personal data, SkillPrivus flags at least one potential violation in 307 of these skills (54.5%), and its judgments agree with three blind human annotators on 83.2% of resolvable moves. The flagged behaviors include sensitive inferences, information use beyond the stated task, and disclosures to unexpected recipients, suggesting that privacy harms can arise from ordinary skill functionality.
- **Key innovations:**
  - Static privacy audit of agent skills (726 real skills from marketplaces): extracts data operations from code/instructions, grounds them in source evidence, adapts Contextual Integrity; flags violations in 54.5% of personal-data skills; 83.2% agreement with 3 blind human annotators.

### 7.7 AI-Assisted Decision Support for Formula 1 Pit-Stop Strategy: Deep Reinforcement Learning and Mathematical Programming
- **arXiv:** [2611.26909](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26909) [math.OC; cs.AI] — 19 Nov 2026
- **Authors:** Felipe Méndez; Charles Thraves
- **Institution/Company:** Not listed in arXiv metadata
- **Abstract:** Pit-stop strategy in Formula 1 is a sequential decision problem under uncertainty, in which strategists decide at each lap whether to pit and which tire compound to fit. We develop a data-driven simulator, calibrated with historical race data, that captures tire degradation, drivers' recent performance, overtaking, yellow flag events, and rivals' pit-stop decisions. We train agents with five Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) algorithms and compare them with a Quadratic Mixed-Integer Programming (QMIP) formulation that is optimal in deterministic single-driver settings. Without uncertainty or competition, all DRL methods except TRPO achieve near-optimal performance. With a full grid of competitors and stochastic race events, the ranking of DRL methods depends on the evaluation setting: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and its recurrent variant are the only methods that improve on the QMIP both in fully simulated races and when the actual 2024 races are replayed. Averaged over eight circuits, PPO improves on the QMIP by 0.33 and 0.49 positions in the two settings and Recurrent PPO by less, and in the replay PPO finishes 0.66 positions ahead of the drivers' actual strategies simulated in the same races. The QMIP performs similarly to the other methods, even though it cannot anticipate yellow flag events. Finally, we use the trained PPO policy as a look-ahead decision-support tool that shows the strategist the distribution of race outcomes under each candidate pit-stop action. Applied retrospectively to eight 2024 races, the look-ahead identifies the laps where the actual decision, the recommendation, or a third action was preferable.
- **Key innovations:**
  - Data-driven F1 pit-stop simulator (tire degradation, driver form, yellow flags, rivals' stops); 5 DRL algorithms + QMIP; PPO/Recurrent PPO best under uncertainty/competition (+0.33/+0.49 positions in simulated/replayed races).
  - Look-ahead decision-support tool: shows strategist the distribution of race outcomes under each candidate pit-stop action; identifies optimal laps in 8 replayed 2024 races.
